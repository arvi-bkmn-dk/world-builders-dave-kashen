# What 15 Triathlons Taught Me About Building Teams

## Summary

Dave found his model of team culture in an amateur endurance program, not at Goldman Sachs or the hedge fund. Depressed after moving to the Bay Area and a breakup — *"taking walks every day from the office, calling my mom, just crying"* — he joined **Team in Training**, the Leukemia and Lymphoma Society's program. *"I had no idea it would parallel the type of team culture I'd later help the world's best founders build."*

The diagnosis of what companies get wrong: **high corrective activity, low supportive and appreciative activity.** Three reasons — it feels *more intimate, therefore scarier*, to appreciate than to correct; a fear that support costs motivation; and keeping people at arm's length in case you have to let them go.

But the fix isn't volume. **"The difference isn't the amount of feedback or celebration. It's the underlying frame."** Absent a felt shared goal, *"feedback feels like judgment. Appreciation feels performative. Accountability feels adversarial"* — interactions turn **transactional instead of transformational.**

What made Team in Training different: unambiguous intent behind every interaction (*"you knew it was coming from someone who wanted you to succeed"*), **individual goals nested inside a collective mission**, and cultural symbols — purple jerseys, *"Go team!"* shouted between strangers at races.

Four moves to copy it, including the **5:1** appreciation-to-correction ratio, with standards explicitly unchanged: *"the technical feedback still needs to be precise."*

Closing question: **"When your team gets feedback, do they hear 'you're being judged' or 'we want you to win'?"**

## Notable Quotes

> In most companies, there's a default norm: high corrective activity, low supportive and appreciative activity.

> We're weirdly shy about celebrating, appreciating, and cheering each other on.

> It can feel more intimate (therefore scarier) to genuinely appreciate someone than to give them constructive feedback.

> The difference isn't the amount of feedback or celebration. It's the underlying frame.

> When the goal isn't felt to be shared by all parties, interactions begin to feel transactional instead of transformational.

> You knew it was coming from someone who wanted you to succeed.

> We were literally in it together.

> Clean fuel (belief, support, celebration) is more effective than dirty fuel (fear, criticism, pressure) at taking people beyond their limits.

> The technical feedback still needs to be precise. The standards still need to be high.

> The most neglected fact in business is that we're all human.

> When your team gets feedback, do they hear you're being judged or we want you to win?
