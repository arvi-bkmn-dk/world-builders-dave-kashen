# What If They Realize I'm in Over My Head

## Summary

Opens on the hidden labour of looking competent: the over-crafted investor update, the rehearsed board answers, the curated LinkedIn confidence — "all to avoid one terrifying moment: someone discovering they don't actually have all the answers."

Dave's move is to deny the premise. **Being in over your head is literally the job description**, and *"if you have any kind of ambition, you are, by definition, an imposter."* The trap is believing you shouldn't be, which runs a cycle ending in eroded trust and an unused team.

The origin story is school: right answers brought praise, wrong ones brought shame — but **in school they taught us the material before the test.** Founders then expect themselves to know answers to problems that may have no known solution, because worthiness got sourced from being the one with the answers.

The counter-objection is handled at length — *"if I drop the image, everything I've built will crumble"* — with the observation that an image that works still doesn't get you loved for who you are, plus **"if you feel like you have to pretend in order to feel safe, that's not safety at all."**

A four-step exit follows: identify the pattern, name what it's designed to give you, get present and notice you can feel that now, and welcome the feelings the pattern was avoiding — *"feelings aren't actually painful. Resistance to feelings is painful."*

Ends with the paradox: people follow the leader who admits not knowing over the one who pretends. *"Your team already knows you don't have all the answers. They're just waiting for you to stop pretending so they can actually help."*
