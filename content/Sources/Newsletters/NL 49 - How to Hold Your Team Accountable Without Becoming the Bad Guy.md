# How to Hold Your Team Accountable Without Becoming the Bad Guy

## Summary

Founders arrive confused about how accountability fits a conscious culture. They've worked in places where "missing a goal meant you were bad and wrong. Where the boss stood over you like some tyrannical widget-factory manager." So they **throw the baby out with the bathwater** — and "throwing out accountability throws out performance too."

The diagnosis: most people **lump the accounting together with the response**, so any missed commitment automatically implies blame, shame or punishment. The core shift is to pull them apart. The accounting is neutral and factual — *we said $4M, we got $3.6M* — and the response is a separate, context-dependent decision, which can include letting someone go **without** making them bad or wrong.

Underneath sits an explicit team agreement about what integrity means: **90% do what you said, 9% renegotiate as soon as you know, 1% acknowledge and clean up.** With that in place, "accountability becomes an act of service."

Agreements themselves need three parts — **who will do what by when** — because "it's way harder to hold people accountable for something you told them to do than for something they agreed to do."

And a clarification on 100% responsibility: it is not self-blame. The leader asks *how did I contribute — did I fail to set clear expectations, did I see we were off-track and stay silent?*
