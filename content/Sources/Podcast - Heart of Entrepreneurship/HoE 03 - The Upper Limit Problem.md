# HoE 03 — The Upper Limit Problem

## Summary

The episode opens on Andrew needling Dave for skiing four or five days a week while trying to build something serious, and Dave names it as a trade-off mentality — the belief that you must sacrifice what you love to achieve your vision. That sets up the episode's real subject: not how much you can have, but how much you will *let yourself* have.

The centrepiece is the **Upper Limit Problem**, which Dave credits to Gay and Katie Hendricks' *The Big Leap*. A homeostatic thermostat for how good life is allowed to get — and when you exceed it, your system finds a way to bring you back down. Jesse's telling of being told he had one is the best part: he was the most ambitious person he knew, with no ceiling, which is precisely why the diagnosis landed as an insult. The setting isn't about ambition; it's about tolerance for things being good.

Underneath it runs the reversal that makes the whole podcast cohere: *you're at this level of success because you're experiencing this level of joy*, not the other way round. Dave extends it to worthiness — everyone is inherently worthy of what they authentically want, so one way to "deserve" what you want is to drop the belief that you don't. The Mexican fisherman parable and the **do-have-be** critique carry the same point.

The practical half is about wants. Andrew can't go for a run without dragging guilt along, and Dave's diagnosis is **feel over figure** — credited to Joe Hudson — that choices only present as hard decisions when we're scared, and the mind's figuring is a strategy for avoiding the feeling. It closes on **have-to versus get-to** (Dave's taxes) and points ahead to the **Zone of Genius**.

## Notable Quotes

> You're giving voice to a trade-off mentality that most people believe — that I can't have it all, I have to sacrifice the things I love in order to achieve my vision.

> Are you willing to have what you want?

> Human beings have one and only one problem in life. It's the upper limit problem, and we have lots of symptoms of that problem.

> It's this homeostatic setting for how good life can be, how good we can feel.

> People interpret it as: you're experiencing that joy because you're at this level of success — as opposed to you're at this level of success because you're experiencing this level of joy.

> Everyone is inherently worthy of what they authentically want.

> What you're doing is what you're committed to. What you're getting is what you're committed to.

> Most people choose to do the thing and resist doing the thing at the same time.

> The times when these choices occur to us as hard decisions is when we're scared.

> All of it is just a strategy to avoid feeling the fear.

> You don't have to pay taxes. There are consequences to not paying your taxes, and most people — myself included — prefer the consequences of paying them.
