# Every part of you has an intended positive outcome

> Every part of you has an intended positive outcome. To the point — there's no bad parts. It's not trying to sabotage you. It's not trying to get in the way.

Which makes the useful question *what is this for*, not *how do I stop it*.

The stakes are in how we usually treat the parts we dislike:

> The way most of us have learned to relate to ourselves is like — we're bad and we need to be controlled. Oh, I gotta stamp out that part, I gotta make the liar wrong, shove it down deep, not see it, not embrace it.

And suppression doesn't remove it:

> Ironically, these parts tend to leak out in unhealthy ways. I'm not a jerk, I'm a nice guy — so I buried deep down the really aggressive assertive one. But then if I got really upset, I might get really triggered, and then I really am a jerk.

The alternative is specific and hard: "can I love the one in me who wants to lie because I'm scared?"

Jesse's account of doing this with a therapist gives the texture — the anxious part talks back, and it is angry: *"dude, you pretended like I didn't exist for 30 years. And here I've been working my ass off trying to keep you on the straight and narrow."* Asked what it needed to feel safe, it said: for the judging part to stop judging it. Two parts, one of them policing the other.

Dave's close: "anytime we're judging, we're just avoiding feeling something we don't want to feel."
