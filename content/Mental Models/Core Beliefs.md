# Core Beliefs

## What It Is

Dave's answer to Jesse's question — *if you only had 30 minutes to change someone's life as much as possible, what would you do?* The answer was specific and it is effectively his theory of leverage:

> Every person has 1 to 3 core beliefs. And a belief is really just a fancy way of saying deeply held story. It's not a fact.

→ [[A belief is just a deeply held story]] · [[One to three core beliefs shape everything]]

## The Model

1. **Drill down to the core belief.** Not a preference or an opinion — the load-bearing one, usually of the form *X equals Y* where Y is love, safety or worth.
2. **Show that the opposite is just as true.** Explicitly *not* to convince them to adopt the opposite: "just so that it's neutral — it's not a fact." The goal is to demote it from reality to story. → [[Asking how the opposite could be true loosens the grip without requiring you to be wrong]]
3. **Negotiate to the increment if the whole won't move.** When the full claim is too threatening, find the version the body will accept.

## How Dave Uses It

Jesse's own session is the worked example, and it took longer than thirty minutes.

His core belief was **money equals love** — with money equals security and money equals success stacked around it, but the root being "the more money I make, the more I will get love." Dave's move: *you could lose all your money and have just as much love in your life, or just as much security.*

> No way — like I could feel my body going like this in that conversation.

The body's refusal is the confirmation that the real belief has been found. Dave didn't let him off the hook, but he did negotiate: they moved to *incrementally more money would not make me more loved or more safe*, which Jesse could get on board with. That was the life-changing increment.

Note what it had been generating: "if I'm not a billionaire, I won't be worthwhile." A belief about money producing a verdict about worth, showing up everywhere in his life.

Dave also notes where such beliefs come from is "not all that important, somewhat obvious" — the work is the loosening, not the archaeology.

### What a belief is

Anaïs Nin, quoted: *"we don't see things as they are, we see them as we are."* → [[We don't see the world as it is, we see the world as we are]] · [[Beliefs determine what you notice and filter out]]

### Why they're self-fulfilling

His two-children example is the cleanest statement of the mechanism in his work. A child whose needs are met reliably forms *people are reliable*; one whose needs aren't forms *people can't be counted on.*

→ Human beings would rather be coherent and right than create what they really want

### The common core beliefs

**About self:** *I'm incapable of ___ · I'm too ___ · I'm unworthy / not good enough / unlovable · there's something wrong with me · I'm shy / not good with people · I'm different / don't belong · I need to take care of everyone · I have to do it all myself · I'm special / better than everyone else*

**About others:** *people are out to take advantage of me · people are greedy and selfish · people can't be trusted · people will disappoint me*

**About the world:** *it's dangerous out there · good guys finish last · the tallest blade of grass gets cut · the universe is indifferent*

### Six ways to revise one

Often *"simply surfacing a belief that has outlived its usefulness and bringing it into consciousness is enough."* Otherwise:

1. **Declare it released** — say it out loud, ideally to others. *"I'm letting go of the belief that…"* As done in the Landmark Forum. Expect to repeat it: beliefs are *"particularly well-worn neural pathways that have been firing together for a long time."*
2. **Benefits and costs** — the costs are obvious; the **benefits** are not, and seeing them stops you making yourself wrong for holding it. *"No part of you is trying to sabotage you."* → [[Every part of you has an intended positive outcome]]
3. **How is the opposite true?** — Byron Katie's *The Work.* → [[Asking how the opposite could be true loosens the grip without requiring you to be wrong]]
4. **Apply the belief to others** — *"what about this person who's accomplished a bit less than you? Are they unworthy?"* → Apply the belief to someone else and see if it holds up
5. **See yourself without it** — *"when you no longer believe you're unworthy, what will be possible?"*
6. **Rewrite history** — an NLP exercise: step into the body of the youngest version of you who held the belief, have your adult self tell them what you now know, then run the timeline forward with the revised belief integrated.

**And the tone it must be done in:**

> Remember, **what you resist persists.**

## Demonstrated Live — the belief loop closing

→ [[We experience what we believe, not the other way around]] · [[Your outer results are a reflection of your inner world]]

Dave then puts the causal question directly, and it is the whole model in one line:

**Two moves for getting underneath a belief.** First, the feeling that the belief is protecting you from — because that's what holds it in place:

→ [[What am I afraid would happen if I let go of this belief]] · Your results are limited by which feelings you are unwilling to feel

→ [[What are the benefits of the behavior you want to change]]

**The recursion this exposes.** A belief that money buys freedom builds its own cage, and the fear of releasing it is what keeps it:

→ [[Follow the impulse, not the compulsion]] · [[Most of what we want is a substitute for what we really want]]
