# The Upper Limit Problem

## What It Is

Credited by Dave to Gay and Katie Hendricks (*The Big Leap*), and framed in the strongest possible terms:

> Human beings have one and only one problem in life. It's the upper limit problem — and we have lots of symptoms of that problem.

It is a homeostatic setting for how good life is allowed to get and how good you are allowed to feel. Exceed it and the system, like a thermostat doing its job, finds a way to bring you back down. → [[The upper limit problem is a thermostat for how good life is allowed to get]]

## The Model

**1. The setting is about tolerance, not ambition.** This is why Jesse rejected the diagnosis outright — he was the loudest, most ambitious person in the room, building a billion-dollar business, no ceiling. Dave: *no, you have it, and everyone has it.* Ambition can be unlimited while the tolerance for things actually being good stays low.

**2. Exceeding it feels like threat.** Life going too well is unfamiliar, and unfamiliar registers as unsafe.

**3. The system corrects.** The symptoms run from subtle to catastrophic:
- **A thought** — waiting for the other shoe to drop. "What if this doesn't work out." "I should be doing more."
- **Manufactured worry** — Hendricks' own example: daughter launched happily to college, so he invents a story that she's lonely and calls her in a panic. She's fine. "Everything's just so good, but my system is bumping up against the limit."
- **Structural self-sabotage** — lottery winners back at the same net worth and no happier five years on. The windfall overwhelms the physiology, so the behaviour arrives to spend it back down.

**4. The game becomes raising the limit.** Which is an inside-out job, not an outside-in one. As the limit rises, life gets better — which is the reversal the whole episode turns on. → [[You are at this level of success because you are experiencing this level of joy]]

## How Dave Uses It

To reinterpret an apparent outer-game problem as an inner-game one. Jesse's upper limit showed up in both registers simultaneously: he wasn't experiencing peace or joy despite having hit nearly every goal on a PowerPoint he'd made in college — *and* he was capping his outer success, running a business he openly disliked for years without leaving.

Dave's cut through that second layer is the sharpest line of the episode: *"Okay, well, why are you still spending all of your day and night working on it?"* → [[What you are getting is what you are committed to]]

The stated answer in Hendricks' book, and the episode's destination: live in your **zone of genius**.

## The Book Chapter — Hendricks' four beliefs

> Human beings actually have **one and only one problem**, which he calls the **Upper Limit Problem.** It is our **homeostatic setting for well-being** — it determines just how good life can be and how good we can feel.

Hendricks' own example: a perfect day — sun shining, work he loves, having just dropped his youngest daughter at a great college — interrupted by the thought *I wonder if she's lonely or having trouble making friends.* Recognising it as the mechanism, *"he watched the thought go by and returned to his perfect day."*

### The four beliefs underneath it

1. **I am fundamentally flawed** — and therefore can't, or don't deserve to, thrive.
2. **It's better to play small** than to go for it and fail.
3. **If I'm too happy or successful, I'll be alone or abandon my roots** — by breaking an unwritten rule about how well things can go for people in my family or community.
4. **The more successful I am, the more of a burden I'll be.** I must not expand to my full potential, because if I do I'll outshine others.

Dave frames raising it as the highest-leverage belief change available: *"you could think of raising your Upper Limit as **the central belief change that matters to your well-being** — hence the author's description of it as the one and only one problem we have."*
