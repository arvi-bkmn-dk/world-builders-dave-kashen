# The Sailboat and the Holes

## What It Is

Dave's answer — credited to coach Brian Franklin — to the strengths-versus-weaknesses question every founder eventually asks. Andrew's version of it:

> There's a part of me thinking: if I'm not good at this, why am I even spending any time on it? Why don't I just spend time on the things I'm good at and screw the rest?

## The Model

You're the captain of a sailboat.

- **The sail** is your strength. Getting it right is the main focus, and it's what moves the boat.
- **Holes below the waterline** are weaknesses severe enough to sink you. If the boat has holes, you spend all your time bailing and never trim the sail at all.

> You've got to get the holes fixed so you're not spending all your time bailing out the water. Get it just up to the point that there's no more holes in the boat — and then go focus on your strengths.

The threshold is the whole model. Weaknesses get worked on **only up to seaworthiness**, never to excellence. "Just up to the point" is a deliberately low bar, and it's what resolves the tension: focus on strengths is right, and it presumes the boat floats.

## How Dave Uses It

As a resolution rather than a compromise. The popular advice (*only play to your strengths*) and the dutiful advice (*shore up your weaknesses*) are both wrong as stated; the question is only ever whether a given weakness is above or below the waterline.

Andrew's case is the illustration: he isn't good at scheduling and planning ahead, and had written it off as something to route around. But disorganisation was producing the [[The Overwhelm Recipe|overwhelm]] that consumed the attention he wanted for his strengths. That's a hole, not a missing flourish — he was bailing.

Note how it pairs with [[Experiments Instead of Failure]]. The sailboat says which weaknesses are worth addressing; the experiment frame is how you address one you've already concluded you can't fix.
