# Both parts want the same outcome and have different strategies

Dave's method for a founder stuck on a decision, which he says is where parts work comes up most.

The presentation is always the same shape — *part of me wants to take the acquisition offer, part of me wants to raise and see how big this can be* — and the founder reads it as a defect. "Especially entrepreneurs, people used to getting accolades for being smart and knowing the answer: oh no, I'm confused, and confused is bad."

Dave treats it as a negotiation instead, and the lightweight version is just to let the parts speak:

> The part of you that wants to go for it — what does it want for you? Oh yeah, I want to see what I'm made of, I want to be courageous. Okay, the part that does want to sell — oh, I'm tired, I want a break.

He maps it onto interest-based negotiation: stop at the **position** ("I want X, I want Y") and it's a deadlock; surface the **interest** and a third option usually exists. "Maybe I can take a sabbatical, or maybe I can take a secondary and take some money off and relax a little."

The unlock is that the parts are not actually opposed:

> If you ask what's the intended positive outcome, they'll both say — I want Jesse to be happy and fulfilled. We're on the same team here. We just have different strategies we've learned or believe will work to meet that outcome.

Dave adds a layer worth having: usually both parts are below the line and **not trusting each other**. The one who wants to sell doesn't trust that going for it will be fulfilling; the one who wants to go for it doesn't trust he won't feel he sold out. Naming the distrust is often what resolves it.
