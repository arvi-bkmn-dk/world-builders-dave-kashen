# Experiments Instead of Failure

## What It Is

A reframe Dave credits to Joe Hudson, aimed at a specific neurological obstacle rather than at attitude.

> There's a part of our brain that's responsible for us not failing. Think about a lion that tries to become the dominant lion and gets beaten down — don't do that again, you're going to get killed.

The consequence is that **coding something as a failure recruits machinery against retrying it.** Not discouragement; protection. → [[Run experiments so that the experiment fails instead of you]]

## The Model

**1. Frame the attempt as an experiment, not a resolution.** Not *I'm finally going to stop eating sweets* but *what happens if I try this?*

**2. Let the experiment be the thing that fails.** The grammatical shift is the whole mechanism:

> The experiments either fail or don't fail. It's not "I'm a failure." It's: oh, that experiment failed. Next.

**3. Run many.** Andrew's worked example, on getting his phone away from his bed: what happens if I charge it in the other room? In a lockbox? With screen time off? "If I try these 7 experiments or 50 experiments, I get to see which ones work."

**4. Expect to have written yourself off.** Andrew's admission is the reason this needs a model at all:

> I had a block in my brain that told me that there's certain things that I just can't do. These are things I've accepted that I'm not good at, and I've shifted my attention away from them.

One failed attempt had closed the category. Dave's instruction — *try a few other options* — sounds embarrassingly obvious, and Andrew notes that it took relationship leverage before he could hear it.

**5. The supporting evidence: the marshmallow test.** → [[The marshmallow test measured strategies, not willpower]]

## How Dave Uses It

Jesse's Wednesday work week is the model at full scale: noticing he wasn't enjoying the year, he spent no time diagnosing blame, made a couple of observations, and changed the structure — explicitly as a trial. "We may get a month or two in and go, it's not really working. I'm going to try something and we're going to see what happens."

Dave's highlight of what Jesse *didn't* do:

> He didn't try to figure out the right answer, which is what most people do. He thought: what do I want? What would be most enjoyable? People way undervalue their authentic wants.
