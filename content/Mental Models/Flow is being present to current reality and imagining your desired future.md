# Flow is being present to current reality and imagining your desired future

Dave defines flow as two things held at once:

> 1. **Present to current reality** — not resisting it, not wishing things were different. Accepting things as they are right now.
> 2. **Imagining your desired future** — seeing clearly where you want to go.

> When you're doing those two things at once, the **cognitive dissonance** between how things are now and how you imagine them to be in the future generates this creative flow where ideas are coming through.

> You're in a state where you're both highly productive and experiencing high joy and creativity.

The two exits from flow are exactly the two ways of dropping one of the halves:

- **Frustration** — collapsing the desired future into the present. *"Why haven't I yet built that successful company? Why is my team not moving faster?"* You lose the acceptance half. → [[Frustration is a desired future collapsed into the present]]
- **Fear** — focusing on the **undesired** future. *"If we don't move faster, we'll fail."* You lose the desired-future half. His image: **"like driving a motorcycle looking at the wall instead of the road. You're going to hit the wall."**

External triggers — a churned customer, a resignation, conflict — pull you out through resistance. But Dave is careful that nothing needs to go wrong:

> Even when nothing's going wrong externally, your mind still drifts. It's scanning for problems and will even **invent** them to try to solve.

He gives himself as the example, the Monday morning after Thanksgiving: *"my mind started scanning: what's wrong? Some of them weren't even problems, just decisions I needed to make. But my mind labeled them as threats."*

> They're radars constantly scanning for danger.

The practice is therefore not to stop drifting but to **notice** the drift, return to accepting things as they are, and refocus on the future you're creating.
