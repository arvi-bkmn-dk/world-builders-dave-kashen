# Separate the accounting from the response

Dave's account of why accountability feels like blame:

> Most people lump the **accounting** together with the **response**. Anytime someone didn't do what they said they would do, there's an automatic assumption that something negative needs to happen. Blame. Shame. Punishment.

So founders who don't want that culture throw out accountability entirely — "hoping trust and good intentions will be enough." And **"throwing out accountability throws out performance too."**

The shift:

> The accounting process can be completely **neutral** — just the facts. What did we say we would do? What actually happened?

> Your sales team committed to $4 million and hit $3.6 million. Those are just facts. No drama needed.

Only then does the second question arise — *what's the appropriate response?* — and it is "wildly context-dependent." Hitting 80% after losing half the team might warrant a raise. A third consecutive miss on an achievable target might mean the wrong person in the role —

> But here's the nuance: you can make that decision **without any blame.** They're not a bad person. They're just not currently able to do this particular thing. That's a fact-based assessment, not a judgment.

> The purpose of the response should always be **learning and improvement**, not assigning blame.

When the threat of blame is gone, "people stop hiding. They stop getting defensive. They actually get curious about what went wrong because they want to get better."
