# One Hundred Percent Responsibility

## What It Is

The first of the 15 Commitments of Conscious Leadership, and in Jesse's assessment "arguably the most important." Dave reads both halves verbatim, and the below-the-line version is what makes it precise:

> **Above the line:** I commit to taking full responsibility for the circumstances of my life and my physical, emotional, mental and spiritual well-being. I commit to support others to take full responsibility for their lives.

> **Below the line:** I commit to blaming others and myself for what is wrong in the world. I commit to be a victim, villain or hero, and take more or less than 100% responsibility.

Note that **more** than 100% is also below the line. Over-owning is not a virtue here; it's the hero position, and it sits alongside victim and villain.

## The Model

**1. It is not magical thinking.** The standard objection — *I can't control the weather, or someone hitting my car* — is conceded immediately:

> You can't control the weather. But you can control your experience of the weather.

Two people on the same rollercoaster: one gripping the bar with cortisol coursing through them, one going *woohoo*. Identical conditions, opposite experience. → [[You cannot control the weather but you can control your experience of it]]

**2. Responsibility is not blame.** This is the distinction Dave says matters most in practice, because most people substitute one for the other. It routes off the same fork as The Responsibility Roadmap: the moment you call a situation a *problem*, the mind asks who's to blame.

**3. Self-blame counts as blame.** The "buck stops here" move looks maximally responsible and functions as an exit:

> Okay, I know I'm the CEO, I'm supposed to say it's my fault. Okay, it's my fault. But they don't actually look at: well, how did I create the conditions to have a VP of engineering who keeps missing deadlines?

→ [[Saying it is my fault is usually a way to end the conversation]]

**4. See yourself as the creator of the results.** Then ask *how am I creating this* — and keep asking, because the layers go deep. Dave's worked chain: I'm telling myself this story · I'm imagining this thing · I overcommitted to these people · why did I do that? · I wanted them to like me · I wanted to look good and avoid looking bad.

## How Dave Uses It

Jesse's testimony is that it is *lighter*, not heavier, which is counterintuitive:

> In some ways it's so much more light, because you don't actually spend that much time trying to find the problem — which I think so many people are programmed to find.

Noticing he wasn't enjoying the year, he spent zero time diagnosing blame and went straight to *how am I creating this*, then redesigned his week. → [[Experiments Instead of Failure]]
