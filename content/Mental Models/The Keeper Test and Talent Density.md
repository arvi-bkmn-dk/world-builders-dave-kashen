# The Keeper Test and Talent Density

> [!info] Not Dave's model — attributed

## What It Is

Dave's answer to *"should I let this person go?"* — one of the most common issues he hears.

His reason is about the threshold rather than the person:

## The Model

**Talent density**, and the story behind it. In the 2000s recession Netflix laid off 30–40% of the team:

→ [[Your lowest performing people bring down your highest performing people]] · [[Keeping someone who is not a fit is a lose lose]]

Dave's flat rejection of the usual defence:

## Why It Is Built This Way — the inner-game half

→ [[Heroes require victims]] · [[Fight is villain, flight is hero, freeze is victim]]

And the question he uses to dismantle it:

> What if I offered to go back in your life and **take away all of your setbacks, all your failures?** Would you let me?

→ [[Would you let me take away all your setbacks and failures]]

Plus the observation that the status quo isn't kind either:

> If someone was **thriving** in the role, you wouldn't be thinking about letting them go. So it **can't actually feel great for them**, if they're self-aware, to be in a role where they're not thriving.

He is careful about the posture this licenses: *"I'm not saying you want to go to them and think **oh, I'm doing you a favour.**"* Only that, internally, you can stop counting it as pure harm — and then *"with dignity, with respect for the person, let them go."*

## Also in [[NL 74 - The Single Biggest Lever for Scaling Your Company]]

The newsletter adds the Netflix story and a sharper test of the answer:

> During the 2008 financial crisis, Netflix laid off somewhere between thirty and forty percent of their team. Absolute company performance improved.

> The distinction that matters here, and this is worth slowing down on, is whether your answer is about the person or the situation.

Wanting to keep someone because of the gap, the search or the extra work is fear. Wanting to keep them because they're extraordinary is the test.

> A players hire A players, B players hire C players.
