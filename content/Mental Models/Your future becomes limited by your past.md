# Your future becomes limited by your past

Jesse describes mistaking self-description for self-awareness: *"I remember considering myself self-aware because I could describe my personality honestly… and in some ways it's a straitjacket."*

Dave's reply is the mechanism:

> By definition, **your future becomes limited by your past.** Because if you're using **evidence from the past** to determine who you are, then **you can never become anything more than who you were.**

> That's the trap a lot of people fall into. *Clearly I am — I can't do that, because I've never done it.* Well, **you never did anything until you first did it.**

> As we get older it calcifies — *I'm this kind of a person, I'm not this kind of a person* — and then we just gather **self-confirming evidence.**

His own case makes it concrete: *"I was an investment banker, I was a hedge fund. If I thought **I am an investor** and that's how I identified myself, then the possibility of becoming a coach wouldn't have made sense."*

> Almost everything we experience is a result of **self-fulfilling beliefs.**

> I believe any human — other than certain physical feats — **could basically create what any other human could create, if they just had the same consciousness and set of beliefs.**

→ [[You could behave any way any other human behaves]] · [[When being meets doing, being wins]] · [[Core Beliefs]]

The second cost of a fixed identity is that it becomes the **source of your value**, which turns ordinary setbacks into existential ones. *"I am worthy because I am a successful entrepreneur — so the moment that feels threatened, it feels like an existential threat."* Jesse's example is the person laid off after a decade who goes into a tailspin: not arrogant, not high-ego, but identified. → [[Equating your value to the value of your company is a major trap]]
