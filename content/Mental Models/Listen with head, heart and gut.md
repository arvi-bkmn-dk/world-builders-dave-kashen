# Listen with head, heart and gut

A three-layer structure for listening, against the default Dave names first:

> Leaders often listen just enough to jump in with advice and try to fix the problem. They rarely discover the deeper issue because they're busy crafting a response.

Two costs, and the second is the one leaders miss: "when you listen to fix, it keeps you from fully listening and leaves the other person not feeling fully heard" — and it "creates dependence on us, where team members keep coming to us expecting answers instead of learning to trust their own judgment."

The three layers:

| | The question |
|---|---|
| **Head** | what did they literally say? |
| **Heart** | what is the emotional undercurrent — worried, upset, eager? |
| **Gut** | what do they actually want? |

Then reflect all three back before responding:

> I hear that you don't think the timeline is realistic *(Head)*, and it sounds like you're feeling some fear around it *(Heart)*. I imagine you really want to align on a timeline that you feel confident in *(Gut)*. Is that right?

> This simple step often defuses tension, ensures the other person feels heard, and helps uncover the real issue.

The **Gut** layer is what separates this from active listening. Guessing at the want — and being willing to be wrong out loud — is what surfaces the thing the conversation was actually about. → [[The presenting issue is almost never the real issue]]
