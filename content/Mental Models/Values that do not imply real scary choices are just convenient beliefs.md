# Values that do not imply real scary choices are just convenient beliefs

The test for whether a stated value is a value.

> Values that don't imply real, scary choices aren't really values — they're just convenient beliefs.

A value only becomes visible when it costs something. *Respect* on a wall costs nothing. *Respect* when your best-performing VP is the one violating it, weeks before a Series A, is a value.

Dave's procedure for finding your actual ones, since "most founders have never clarified their authentic values" — and he insists company values are downstream: **your company's values are always an extension of your personal values.**

> **The People You Choose** — look at your closest relationships and the people you've most loved working with. What do you most value about them?
> **Peak Experience Analysis** — when did you feel most energized and alive? What values were you honoring?
> **The Anti-Values Mirror** — what behaviors in others make you most angry? Your anger triggers reveal your deepest values.
> **The "No Fear" Test** — what would you do if you weren't afraid? Fear often masks our true values.

The third is the projection tooling pointed at values — anger as information about what you hold, not just what you reject. → [[Anger is boundary energy and a form of love, not its opposite]] · [[Anything you judge in yourself or in other people is a shadow]]

Then: narrow to 3–5, rate yourself 1–10 on each, and **identify the risks worth taking** — "what would you be willing to risk to uphold this value? Notice where fear has been stopping you."

And a bottom-up step most leaders skip — ask the team first: *what values, if violated, would cause you to quit?*
