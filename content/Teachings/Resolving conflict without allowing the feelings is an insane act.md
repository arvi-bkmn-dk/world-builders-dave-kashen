# Resolving conflict without allowing the feelings is an insane act

Dave's argument for emotions at work starts from inevitability: conflict will happen, because it is "a group of human beings trying to accomplish things together" with different values and styles. Given that, suppression does not remove it — "it's gonna leak out in different ways."

> Trying to resolve conflict without allowing feelings is kind of an insane act, 'cause it's the feelings that are really creating it.

He names the fear that stops leaders: that welcoming feelings makes things chaotic and distracting, when "we need to be serious, we need to focus, we need to perform." His observation runs the other way:

> Cultures that start to welcome feelings, they actually resolve things much more quickly. People feel more fully seen and alive. They make decisions faster.

His diagnostic example is disagree-and-commit, the thing leaders complain fails: "it's usually 'cause people haven't fully been able to express their feelings around the idea. So they're kind of stuck holding onto this." The commit fails because the disagree was never completed.

Jesse's contribution is the reframe that makes it land with skeptical leaders:

> The feelings are always there, whether you say them or not. You're not controlling how they're actually feeling — whether they share it or not, it's there.

So the choice is not whether the team has feelings but whether they are visible. And the mess people are trying to avoid is produced by the avoidance:

> The thing that creates the drama in the workplace and all the messiness that people are trying to avoid is the resistance to all of it. It's all the management around the emotions — it's not the emotions themselves.

Two practices. **Make space deliberately**: Jesse will say "we're gonna go off on this client in this room for 20 minutes and be mad about this, and then we're gonna move through." And **model it**, which is the lower-cost entry — "I'm feeling a little nervous about this." He notes the existing narrow band: frustration is sayable for men at work in a way that sadness or disappointment is not.
