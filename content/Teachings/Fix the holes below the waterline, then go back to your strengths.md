# Fix the holes below the waterline, then go back to your strengths

Dave's answer, credited to coach Brian Franklin, to the question Andrew has been carrying: if I'm not good at something, why spend any time on it at all?

> You're a sailboat captain. Focusing on the sail and getting that just right — that's your main focus, that's your strength. But if the boat has holes in it and you're constantly bailing out the water, you've got to get the holes fixed so you're not spending all your time bailing.

> Get it just up to the point that there's no more holes in the boat, and then go focus on your strengths.

The model's work is done by the **waterline**, which sorts weaknesses into two categories that get opposite treatment. Above it: ignore, delegate, never improve. Below it: fix to seaworthy and stop.

"Just up to the point" is the operative phrase and it is a deliberately low bar. This is not a case for becoming well-rounded — competence at the hole is never the goal, floating is.

It resolves an argument that only looks like a trade-off. *Play to your strengths* is correct and silently assumes the boat floats; *work on your weaknesses* is correct only for the ones taking on water.

Andrew's scheduling is the case: not a missing flourish but a hole, because the disorganisation generates the [[The Overwhelm Recipe|overwhelm]] consuming the attention he wants for what he's good at. He was bailing. → [[The Sailboat and the Holes]]
