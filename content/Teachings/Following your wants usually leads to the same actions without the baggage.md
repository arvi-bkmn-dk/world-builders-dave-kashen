# Following your wants usually leads to the same actions without the baggage

The honest accounting of what changes when you stop figuring and start following wants — and its honesty is what makes it credible.

> 80% of the time you may do the same thing you were going to do anyway. But you just don't carry the baggage along with you as you do it. And that makes all the difference. You feel light.

Jesse is explicitly *not* claiming better decisions. Most of the time the output is identical. What's removed is the deliberation, the guilt and the divided attention — which turns out to be most of the cost.

This matters because it defuses the objection that following wants is self-indulgent and will wreck your judgment. If the actions barely change, the worry is about a risk that doesn't materialise; what changes is the condition you're in while taking them.

The second-order effect Jesse reports is the one that compounds:

> You also start to find things that arise naturally that capture your attention. The creativity spikes.

Which is the same claim as Dave's throughout — the expansive, present state reaches ideas the hustling state cannot. → [[Motivating a team from vision unlocks ideas hustle cannot reach]]

Andrew's residual objection is fair and goes unresolved: once you get to the want, you still have *is this good for my business* — and limiting beliefs may be sitting underneath that too. Dave concedes it: "You might indeed, Andrew."
