# What you appreciate appreciates

Dave offers this as appreciation's business case, and the pun is the memory device.

> There's this cliché statement — what you appreciate appreciates. And I have found it to be true. If you say to someone, wow, I so appreciate how you went above and beyond on that assignment, I see how detail-oriented you were — what's naturally going to happen is they're going to do more of that.

Jesse's supporting study: a basketball coach showed players video of their mistakes and the team got no better, maybe slightly worse. Showed them both good and bad plays — no real change. Showed **only** what they did well — and the team improved markedly.

Dave's explanation of why this is a leverage point for leaders rather than a nicety:

> We're human and our minds tend toward all the negative stuff, what's wrong, what's not good enough. So we as leaders do that, our team members are all doing that, and everyone's missing all the good stuff.

The negativity bias is running in every head in the building. Constructive feedback adds to a signal that is already saturated; appreciation supplies the one that's missing. → [[Your mind tells stories with problems in them because problems hold attention]]

He's careful not to trade this against candour:

> I don't want to step over candour — it's important for people to be real with each other and give feedback. It's maybe just as, if not more, important to give the positive feedback.

Not less honesty. More of the honest thing nobody says. Dave's evidence for how much it lands: clients who still remember, eleven years later, a boss sitting them down and telling them they were appreciated.
