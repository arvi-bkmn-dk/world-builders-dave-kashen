# What annoys you when others feel it is what you will not feel yourself

The problem with resistance is that it is invisible from the inside. Jesse makes the point plainly: say to someone that they resist their feelings and "they'd be like, what the hell are you talking about?" Practised suppression stops announcing itself — "our defenses are so well formed that we don't even recognise it's happening."

So Dave offers an external diagnostic, credited to Joe Hudson:

> If you wanna know who is not allowed to cry, look at who reacts when babies cry on an airplane.

The logic:

> If I don't let myself feel that feeling, I get annoyed when other people feel the feeling.

Which gives a question you can actually answer about yourself: **what feelings annoy you when other people have them?** That irritation marks the emotion you have taken off your own menu.

Dave names a second flavour: judgment, which works in both directions. Judging someone else's anger points at your own prohibition on anger — and so does judging it in yourself. "Oh, I keep getting angry, what's wrong with me? God, I have a bad temper." Or with sadness: "why am I so weak?" The self-judgment is the same mechanism turned inward.

Jesse adds the version that shows up as a general principle: people who say "people shouldn't be angry" are reporting their own rule, stated as though it were about everyone.

Both tests work because they are observable from outside, where the resistance itself is not.
