# Most of the time we are just recycling thoughts they have already had

Dave's case against advice, and it lands on efficiency rather than etiquette.

> We think we're being helpful when we jump in with solutions, but most of the time **we're just recycling thoughts they've already had.**

The founder in his example had an underperforming team member and had already generated the obvious moves. The group's instinct — *have you tried clearer expectations? weekly check-ins? just fire him* — would have returned him his own list.

> That approach assumes they need more **information.** But most of the time, what we actually need is more **awareness.**

Two different deficits requiring opposite responses. Information is transferable; awareness is not, which is why the advice bounces:

> Real transformation happens from the inside-out. You can give someone the perfect advice, but if they don't see and feel it for themselves — if it doesn't click internally — nothing changes.

> Many times, we already know what we want to do deep down. But what we lack is the **clarity or courage** to act on it.

And a cost most helpers never consider:

> Trying to fix reinforces the idea that there's something wrong with them or their life; that **they are a victim and need your help.**

Which is the hero move in its most generous-looking form. → [[Heroes require victims]] · [[The hero creates temporary relief but undermines learning and growth]]

The alternative is [[CORE Conversations]] — open questions, and the person with the problem doesn't answer.
