# Equating your value to the value of your company is a major trap

The specific form the mistake takes, stated flatly:

> That mistake of equating your value to the value of your company is, I think, a major trap.

It is easy to hold without noticing, because the two numbers move together and everyone around you treats them as one. Valuation up, you are doing well *as a person.* Down, and something is wrong with you.

The cost is not only emotional. It generates the attachment Dave spends his work arguing against — [[The less attached you are to an outcome, the easier it is to create]] — because when the outcome carries your worth, you cannot hold it lightly.

Gagan's lived version is the **burden of proof**. His rose with success rather than falling: at 21 "a successful business, I made a million dollars, that would've been great"; by 25 it was "I'm going to have started two billion-dollar companies by the time I'm 30." Asked what was important about that, the answer underneath was *proving that I was good* — and behind that, a childhood of "external signals from adults and the system that I was good, but not great."

What broke it was not achieving more. It was three years off after Sprig collapsed, discovering "I was so happy doing nothing" — which made the equation impossible to maintain. → [[The burden of proof is an illusion]]

Dave's position, stated as a premise rather than an argument: "we're inherently and born worthy, and the rest is just what we choose to do." → [[Everyone is inherently worthy of what they authentically want]]
