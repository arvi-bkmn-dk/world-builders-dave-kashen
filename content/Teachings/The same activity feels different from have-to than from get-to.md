# The same activity feels different from have-to than from get-to

Dave's sharpening of the point: the context you hold an activity in changes the experience of it while the activity stays identical.

> You can do the literal same activity from a context of *I have to do this*, and it will feel different to you than *I choose to do this*, or *I want to do this*, or *I get to do this*.

His route into it is a coach's line that sounds like a technicality and isn't:

> You don't have to pay taxes. There are consequences to not paying your taxes — and most people, myself included, prefer the consequences of paying them.

Nothing about the obligation changes. What changes is that the payment becomes a preference you hold, which means it belongs to you. "Have to" locates the cause outside you and makes you its object, and that is where the resentment comes from — not from the task.

His worked example is tax preparation, which he genuinely dislikes. For years he did it *and* resisted it, dragging his feet and complaining, making himself and his wife miserable. Then:

> Okay, I'm choosing to do this — so why don't I just do it. Put on some music, she sat next to me, cranked through it in a couple of hours. No big deal.

He's careful not to oversell: he still prefers skiing or coaching. Choosing it didn't make it enjoyable; it removed the second, larger cost layered on top. → [[Most people choose to do a thing and resist doing it at the same time]]
