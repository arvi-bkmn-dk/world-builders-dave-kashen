# Your job is not to have the right answers but to build a team that finds them without you

The second of three things Dave says founders must unlearn, and the one most likely to be defended as competence.

> As an early-stage founder, your ability to make quick, confident decisions kept the company alive. But as your startup grows, **making every decision is a liability, not an asset.** If your team relies on you for all the answers, you've built a fragile system.

*Fragile* is the operative word — it moves the argument off empowerment and onto structural risk. A company that routes every judgment through one person has a single point of failure with a calendar.

> * Replace giving answers with asking better questions.
> * Create a culture where your team owns decisions instead of waiting for you to weigh in.
> * **Stop overvaluing your own thinking** — some of the best solutions will come from others.

The last one is the hardest, because it was accurate for a while. Early on the founder's judgment genuinely was the best available; the error is treating a past fact as a permanent one.

It matches what Dave says elsewhere about listening: leaders who listen in order to fix "inadvertently create dependence, where team members keep coming to us expecting answers instead of learning to trust their own judgment." → [[Listen with head, heart and gut]]

And it is the same structure as the hero problem — rescuing produces relief and prevents learning. → [[The hero creates temporary relief but undermines learning and growth]]
