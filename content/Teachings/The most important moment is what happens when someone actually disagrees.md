# The most important moment is what happens when someone actually disagrees

Dave's opening challenge:

> If everyone on your team agrees with you, what do you need them for?

> Most leaders say they want dissent, then **accidentally punish it** the moment someone actually disagrees.

His three keys:

**1. Ask for it explicitly.** *"What doesn't make sense about this? What's wrong with my thinking here? Why might this not work?"* — and the condition that makes the asking real: "when you're too attached to being right, you get defensive. But when you let go of sourcing your worthiness from having the answers, you can be genuinely open-minded." → [[In school they taught us the material before the test]]

**2. Make it safe to disagree.**

> The most important moment isn't when you ask for dissent, it's **what happens when someone gives it to you.** If you drill into them or get defensive, you've just taught everyone: *this was a trap. Never do that again.*

The alternative is one sentence — *"Thank you so much for bringing that up. I hadn't thought of that"* — on the principle that **"what you appreciate appreciates."** → [[Make a list of your team members and find something to appreciate each week]]

**3. Practice conscious listening** — from head (what are they saying?), heart (what are they feeling?), gut (what do they want?), then repeat it back. → [[Listen with head, heart and gut]] · [[Summarising what someone said and guessing their feelings and wants makes them feel heard]]

> When people feel genuinely heard, they can **disagree with your decision and still commit to executing it.** When they don't feel heard, they won't fully commit.

The structural half of this is naming the decision rule up front — see [[NL 48 - How To Be Collaborative And Decisive At The Same Time]].
