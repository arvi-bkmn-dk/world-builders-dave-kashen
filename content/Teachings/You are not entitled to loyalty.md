# You are not entitled to loyalty

Dave's answer to the founder whose top performer just quit — disappointed, angry, scared, and above all **betrayed**: *"after all the time and energy I put into developing them…"*

> You're **not entitled to loyalty.** You can't guarantee people will stay.

And the argument is symmetry, put uncomfortably:

> Asking "how do I ensure my team stays loyal?" might be backwards, especially when most leaders aren't willing to offer the same loyalty in return. **If times get tough or revenue falls, you'll probably lay people off.** So is it reasonable to expect your team to be more loyal to you than you are to them?

He is careful not to moralise about layoffs — "sometimes they're necessary" — the point is only that "loyalty is a two-way street, and most of us aren't driving both directions."

The replacement question, which changes where your effort goes:

> **"How do I create conditions where people actually want to stay?"** That reframe shifts where you put your energy and attention, which actually increases your chances of keeping your best people.

The first condition is generosity **in advance**:

> Leaders get stingy with appreciation, compensation, and development. Then someone gets a competing offer, and suddenly they're willing to match it. But by then it's too late.

> **People don't stay for the compensation, but they might leave for it.** What they really want is to feel valued.

→ [[Make a list of your team members and find something to appreciate each week]] · [[A lot of leaders look at it as a strategy to keep you jumping through hoops]]

Dave also handles the catastrophising that accompanies a resignation — the certain mass exodus that doesn't come. His questions: *what if the story your mind is making up isn't true? how might the opposite be just as true?* And what actually happens: "most people are fine. Some even step up in surprising ways." → [[Asking how the opposite could be true loosens the grip without requiring you to be wrong]]
