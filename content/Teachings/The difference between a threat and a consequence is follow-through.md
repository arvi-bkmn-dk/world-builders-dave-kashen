# The difference between a threat and a consequence is follow-through

Dave asked Diana Chapman the question directly, because setting boundaries felt like threatening people and he didn't want to threaten anyone.

> Her answer surprised me: **"Follow-through."**

One word, and it relocates the whole distinction. Not tone, not wording, not kindness — whether you actually do the thing.

> A **threat** tries to manipulate: "If you don't stop doing this, I'm going to fire you!" *(even though you know you won't)*

> A **consequence** simply states what's true: "I'm not willing to be spoken to this way. If this continues, I'll end our working relationship."

> Notice the difference? No blame, no reactivity, no attempt to force compliance. **Just clarity about what you will do if they choose certain behaviors.**

A threat is a bluff, and its purpose is to change their behaviour without you having to act. A consequence is a description of your own future conduct, and it requires nothing from them.

Dave names the spiral threats produce, and parents will recognise it:

> Threaten, hope it works, don't follow through with the consequence but increase the threat. It's a downward spiral.

Escalation is what happens when the words were never backed. Each unfulfilled threat teaches that the next one is also empty, so the volume rises while the credibility falls.

The business version: *"I want you to improve your performance"* is a request. *"In order to continue in this role, these specific behaviors must change by this date. If they don't, I'll need to make a change"* is a boundary. → [[Boundaries do not require the other person to do anything]]
