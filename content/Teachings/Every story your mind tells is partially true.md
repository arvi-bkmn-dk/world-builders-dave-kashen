# Every story your mind tells is partially true

Dave calls this a recent and unusually useful realisation, and it maps the two traps on either side of a story.

> Either we hear the story and believe it's wholly true — or we believe it's wholly untrue and we try to fight against it.

Take an inner critic saying *you're weak*. Believe it entirely and you suffer. Fight it — *no, I'm strong* — and you generate resistance, which is its own kind of occupation. Both responses grant the story the status of a claim to be adjudicated.

The third option is to ask *how is it true?* Dave does this on himself, and the answer is almost comically ordinary: sometimes he eats sugar when he's trying not to; he has a frail human body; he used to be able to press 200 pounds and can't now.

> And I'm strong. Right. Like, it's, it's polarities. Right. Both are true.

Once both poles are admitted, there's nothing left to defend, which is why this is the mechanism underneath [[Asking how the opposite could be true loosens the grip without requiring you to be wrong]]. Resistance requires a side.

This is also what Dave offers Andrew when Andrew's discomfort with the word *scared* surfaces — the resistance is to being seen as weak, and the way out isn't proving strength. → [[All humans have all the same parts]]
