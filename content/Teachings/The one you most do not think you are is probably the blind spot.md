# The one you most do not think you are is probably the blind spot

A diagnostic that inverts the obvious one — you locate your least visible role by finding the one you're confident you don't play.

> Amongst those three — hero, victim and villain — the one that you most don't think you are is probably the blind spot.

Dave's own case is the coach who could not see himself rescuing:

> I could really see my victim and villain. But I was like, no no, I don't hero people, I'm a coach. And I realised for so long I would coach from a hero place.

He gives the specific move, with Jesse as the example: Jesse would raise shame about something, and Dave would answer *"no, don't you see, you're such a great crystal — shame is just a story, you don't need to feel that."* His own verdict:

> I think it was actually a disservice.

Talking someone out of a feeling reads as help and functions as rescue — it spares the coach from sitting in it. He caught the pattern *because* it was the one he was sure he didn't have.

Jesse's blind spot was the opposite corner: "I'm never a victim — it felt so not who I was, so opposite. And it turns out there's a really big part of me that is."

Dave's second test is external: the role that "irritates you in other people a lot." Both work on the same logic as [[What annoys you when others feel it is what you will not feel yourself]] — the denied thing is visible from outside, never from inside.
