# The hero creates temporary relief but undermines learning and growth

Why rescuing fails on its own terms, even when it works in the moment.

> The hero creates temporary relief. So okay, everyone feels a little better, but you haven't actually learned, people haven't taken responsibility in the sense of — oh, I see how I created this.

Dave itemises who doesn't get to learn. The salesperson never faces *I said I wanted to hit my quota and I missed it, what do I need to do differently.* The sales manager never faces *how did I co-create this.* So:

> You don't fundamentally resolve the issue, so you'll end up repeating the cycle.

Jesse's addition is that the missed learning is usually subtler than the obvious one. Not just *they didn't make enough calls*, but: the salesperson didn't speak up when they saw the goal made no sense; the sales leader took the founder's number without reconciling it. "Candor is missing, or feeling a feeling was missing" — and those go unexamined precisely because the band-aid worked.

There is also an emotional cost Dave names last, and it is the savior's actual motive:

> The real underlying motivation of the savior tends to be — I don't want anyone to feel bad or sad or upset. If the salesperson never gets to feel the disappointment or frustration, they're not gonna connect with what's actually true for them.

Rescuing removes the feeling that would have produced the change. → [[Unfelt feelings are the glue that keeps unwanted patterns in place]]
