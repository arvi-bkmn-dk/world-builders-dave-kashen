# Sometimes the most courageous thing to do is to let go

Dave's correction of the startup world's most celebrated virtue, and it turns on a survivorship error.

The case: a client whose company was on the brink years ago. Shame and fear came up, he *"hustled and grinded and found a way to get money and keep this thing going."* By the usual standard, a success — *"we give him the trophy because he persisted."* His own reflection later:

> I bet if I had just **let this thing fail** — it was three, four years ago — I probably would've been **ahead of the game in AI.** I see all these people, less experienced, maybe less savvy than me, raising at $10 billion valuations.

Dave's diagnosis of where the lesson goes wrong:

> We extrapolate that **persistence is good** — you should persist, you should endure, that's the good thing. And what we miss is: **the ones that persisted were the ones that actually loved the thing they were doing.** So it wasn't a hardship to persist.

> But we take the lesson that **you should persist no matter what** — when in fact, **sometimes the most courageous thing to do is to let go.**

What letting go actually requires is not resolve but capacity: *"be with the shame and the fear and the disappointment and the heartbreak of letting go, and maybe walking away from the company."* → [[Care deeply and be willing to have your heart broken]] · Your results are limited by which feelings you are unwilling to feel

This is also why the persistence advice is dangerous secondhand. What looked like grit in the survivors was **love for the work**, and prescribing the grit without the love produces exactly the forced, energy-draining version Dave says kills companies. → [[Your authentic wants are the right answer for you]]
