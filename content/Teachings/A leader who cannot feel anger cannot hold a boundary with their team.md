# A leader who cannot feel anger cannot hold a boundary with their team

This is where Dave converts the emotions material into a concrete management diagnosis, and he reports it as the common root beneath a familiar cluster of complaints.

> People just are coming late to meetings. Or how do I get my team to work harder? Or I got this person on my team, they're not performing the way I want. And most of the time if we dig into the root — the leader is not allowing themselves to feel and express anger and say, this is not okay, this is how we do things around here.

The causal chain runs: anger unavailable → boundary unset → the behaviour continues. And critically, the leader usually cannot see it, because the suppressed anger does not present as anger:

> There's a boundary that they want to set, but because they can't access their anger, often they're not even aware of it. They just notice this sort of dull frustration of, oh, why can't I get people to do what I want?

That dull frustration is the tell. It is what anger looks like when it has been denied a direct route.

Dave includes himself, and his self-description is the one most conflict-averse founders would recognise: "for me anger was like a — what? No, I'm just a kind nice guy." What changed with practice was resolution rather than volume:

> I can literally sense a more finely tuned sense of when something I want isn't happening or something I don't want is happening, and then speak to it. So it's a real superpower.

He extends the pattern to sadness: leaders who cannot feel it "won't fire people" and keep too many projects running, because ending things requires grieving them. Jesse confirms it from his own company — he "struggled letting people go a lot," and the honest version was that he "had to literally cry and let go. It didn't work."
