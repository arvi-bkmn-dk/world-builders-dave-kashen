# Saying I am angry turns a passing feeling into an identity

Jesse raises a fear that keeps people from allowing emotions at all, and it is not the fear of the feeling — it is of what the feeling would make them:

> If I allow this, I'll just be an angry person. If I allow this, I'll be a lazy person. If I allow this, I'll just be a sad, depressed person.

Dave locates it in the grammar:

> I am sad, I am angry, I am scared — it's just language, but language is powerful. It's a really different connotation than *I feel sad*. There's an I that's feeling this feeling of sadness right now.

"I am" reports a permanent property. "I feel" reports a passing state and preserves the distinction between the one experiencing and the thing experienced. The construction itself makes a temporary emotion sound pervasive and fixed — "as if I'm an angry person."

Dave's counter is that the premise is wrong anyway: "all humans have all the emotions, whether they know it or not, or are willing to feel it or not." Nobody acquires anger by permitting it. → [[All humans have all the same parts]]

This is the same move his work makes with thoughts. [[Identify as the listener rather than the talker]] separates you from the narrating voice; this separates you from the feeling. In both cases the fix is not to suppress the content but to stop collapsing yourself into it.
