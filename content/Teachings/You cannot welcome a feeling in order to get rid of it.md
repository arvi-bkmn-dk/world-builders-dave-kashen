# You cannot welcome a feeling in order to get rid of it

Dave flags this as the caveat that makes the difference between welcoming and a more sophisticated form of resistance — and admits to years of doing the latter.

> I can't welcome the feeling in order to get rid of it. A lot of clients try that, and man, have I tried that so many times over the years.

His own case is specific. Having had depression for years, sadness arrives carrying a threat — "am I sinking back into depression?" — so his welcome was conditional:

> Okay, welcome sadness. Right — well, you know, welcome *away*.

The welcome is a dismissal wearing the right words, and the feeling registers the difference. He makes the same point in [[HoE 09 - Emotions and the Wisdom in Them]] with the image of a host who says "welcome, come in, come in — okay, get out, get out," letting sadness through only so the real guests can arrive. Tolerating a feeling as a toll on the way to a better one is not allowing it.

The test Dave borrows from Joe Hudson raises the bar past tolerance:

> Could you actually look forward to being sad? Could you look forward to being terrified?

He notes we already do this voluntarily and without difficulty in the right frame — people pay to see scary movies and sad ones. "Some part of us wants to feel that."

The underlying loop is what makes the distinction matter:

> Because we resist them, they feel painful. Because they feel painful, we resist them. So we're stuck in a loop, not realising that we're the one creating it.

A conditional welcome keeps the loop intact, because resistance is still present and it is the resistance that supplies the pain.

## Also in [[NL 57 - Your Humanity Is the Doorway]]

*The newsletter version, where Dave names transactional welcoming as resistance in disguise.*

Dave on his own practice, which is what makes this more useful than the rule it restates:

> For a long time, I was **"welcoming" feelings transactionally.** I'd welcome sadness so it would take its turn and leave, making room for my **"real friends"** — joy and excitement.

> But that wasn't true welcoming. **That was still resistance in disguise.**

The tell is the motive, not the behaviour. The outward action is identical to genuine welcoming; what differs is whether you have a preferred outcome. Doing the practice *in order for the feeling to go* is the practice failing. → [[You cannot welcome a feeling in order to get rid of it]]

The fear underneath his own version:

> I was **terrified that if I truly let sadness in, it would consume me. That it would never end.**

> But here's what I learned: **the feeling itself isn't what's painful. It's the resistance to the feeling that creates suffering.**

> When you actually welcome emotions **without trying to make them leave**, they move through naturally. And when emotions move through instead of getting stuck, **that energy becomes available for everything else.**

The image he returns to:

> **Joy is the matriarch of all the feelings, and if you're not letting her children in, she won't come.** All emotions flow through the same pipe. If you try to block sadness or anger or fear, you're **kinking the pipe**, and joy can't flow through either.

→ [[All emotions run through the same pipe and joy is the matriarch]] · [[The fear that you will drown in a feeling is the lie that keeps it stuck]]

## Also in [[WS 01 - The Inner Keys to Becoming a World-Class Leader]]

After the resist-then-welcome exercise, Dave names the paradox again:

> it's such a paradox because you can't welcome the feeling to make it go away

> But if you truly welcome it, paradoxically, it'll generally start to settle, and you can be with it.
