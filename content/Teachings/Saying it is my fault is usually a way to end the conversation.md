# Saying it is my fault is usually a way to end the conversation

Dave calls this ironic, and it's the most common failure among people genuinely trying to be responsible.

> Most people slap themselves on the wrist — oh, it's my fault, buck stops here, done. Without actually the reflection of how did I create the results being the way they are.

His worked example: a VP of engineering keeps missing deadlines.

> Okay, I know I'm the CEO, I'm supposed to say it's my fault. Okay, it's my fault. But they don't actually look at: well, how did I create the conditions to have a VP of engineering who keeps missing deadlines?

Andrew sees it immediately: "it's almost like a way of trying to shut the conversation down or end it."

Which is exactly what it is. *My fault* has the form of accountability and the function of closure — a payment made so the inquiry can stop. And because it is still blame, only self-directed, it sits below the line in [[One Hundred Percent Responsibility]] alongside blaming anyone else.

The tell is what follows. Real responsibility opens questions (did I set clear expectations? did I avoid the conversation? did I assume they understood?); ritual responsibility closes them.

The alternative Andrew arrives at keeps the situation open without either kind of blame:

> No, this happened. And if we want to ship our product on time, this isn't going to allow us to do that. It's not a problem, it's just what is. Okay, let's think about that and solve it.
