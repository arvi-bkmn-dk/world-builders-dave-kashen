# We each have a favorite drama role and the least favorite is the blind spot

Jesse names the near-universal first mistake with the Drama Triangle:

> The first inclination is always like — *okay, I'm one of those people, **you** are one of those people.* *I'm the victim now, and so-and-so's the villain, and such-and-such is the hero.* And that's **not correct**, right? It's a **thought pattern.**

Dave's correction:

> We tend to **rotate through** those different roles, and the roles have a different energy and mindset to them.

Run as the storyteller playing parts: **victim** — *poor me, this is happening to me, my team is letting me down.* Then the storyteller switches to **villain** — and it often turns inward: *if you were a better CEO, if you were more visionary, you should have hired that person, your investors told you to.* Then **hero** — *it's okay, you did your best*, or *I've already had an exit, I've got plenty of money, I shouldn't be worried about this.*

Dave flags that **spiritual bypass counts as a hero move**: *"it's all good, it just is the way it is — even I was advocating for that, but there's a way you can use that as a hero move and abdicate responsibility."*

Then the diagnostic worth keeping:

> We tend to have **favorites.** And **the one that's our least favorite is usually our blind spot.**

His own case:

> I realised at one point — I definitely go victim, I definitely go villain, but **I don't think I do the hero thing that much.** And then I realised that **for years I was heroing all my clients** — using the tools to help them **avoid feeling shame or sadness**, instead of just allowing them to have their experience.

The role you're confident you don't play is the one running unexamined, and Dave's version of it was indistinguishable from doing his job well.

Jesse's use of it: *"anytime I'm blaming — myself or someone else — I'm like, oof, **I'm in threat right now.**"*
