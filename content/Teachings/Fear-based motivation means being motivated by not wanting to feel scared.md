# Fear-based motivation means being motivated by not wanting to feel scared

Jesse catches an apparent contradiction and asks Dave directly: the podcast keeps saying don't be fear-motivated, and now it's saying follow the fear. *"So which one do I do?"*

Dave's answer is a correction of his own past framing:

> I love that you asked that, 'cause this was a **newer insight** for me. I used to think that way, so I've probably **done some disservice** by speaking that way — as if fear is bad and you should be motivated by inspiration and not fear.

> The issue is **not fear. The issue is resistance to fear.**

And then the precise definition that resolves it:

> When we talk about fear-based motivation, what we **really** mean is: **I'm motivated by not wanting to feel scared.**

> *Fear arises about my company failing. I don't want to feel that fear, so I just whip myself — my inner bully comes out — and I work and I work and I work **to avoid feeling that fear.***

> Which is a very different experience than: *oh God, I'm really scared to fail. Whoa, my heart's racing.* **Welcome fear. Really feel it.** And then once you feel it, you can choose — *okay, I'd like to succeed, what's the next thing to do?*

> **That's how you shift from dirty fuel to clean fuel.**

So the distinction was never fear versus inspiration. It is **felt** fear, which informs and then releases, versus **unfelt** fear, which has to be outrun indefinitely. The grinding isn't caused by the fear; it's caused by refusing it.

Dave also notes fear's discernment problem: telling *wisdom fear* (this is genuinely unsafe) from *old pattern fear* (believing rejection means death) is *"the trickiest one for most founders"* — and that the way you learn to tell them apart is by welcoming fear often enough to know its textures.
