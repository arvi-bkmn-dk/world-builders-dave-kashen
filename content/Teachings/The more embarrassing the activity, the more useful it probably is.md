# The more embarrassing the activity, the more useful it probably is

A rule of thumb with a real mechanism behind it.

Jesse sets it up: *"the more ridiculous that sounds to you, the more you probably need to do it. If you're like — what are you talking about, that's absurd, I'm not angry."* Dave's version:

> I often feel like **the more embarrassing or weird an activity, probably the more useful it is.** 'Cause it's **unfamiliar** — that's why it's uncomfortable. And if it's unfamiliar, it means **you're not doing it much.**

The logic is the same as [[Fear lives at the edge between the familiar and unfamiliar parts of yourself]]: discomfort marks an under-used part of yourself, and the parts you never use are exactly where the unclaimed capacity is. Embarrassment is a proximity alarm for an edge.

Which inverts the usual filter. *That's not really me* and *that feels ridiculous* normally function as reasons to skip something. Here they are the argument for doing it — the reaction is evidence of unfamiliarity, not of unsuitability.

The concrete case is the wiffle-ball bat and the Hoffman Process bashing exercise, where Dave went in convinced he had no anger left and found it *"a couple minutes in."* → [[Move anger with sound and movement in a safe way]]
