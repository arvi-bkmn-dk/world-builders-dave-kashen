# Anytime there is a should involved it is situational awareness distortion

Dave's verdict on management doctrine, delivered through the micromanagement debate.

> Fear tends to put us in this binary mode — like either I'm a micromanager or I'm totally hands off.

He reads the founder-mode discourse as one shame rule replacing another. For years the meme was that micromanagement is bad — "a total should-based, shame-based approach to the conversation" — so founders overcorrected into pure delegation. Then Brian Chesky's essay gave permission, and the rule simply flipped: "founder mode, that's the good thing, letting go is bad, so I should—"

> And the whole point is there is no should. The question that arises if you let go of should is: what do I really want, and what actually best serves this particular situation?

Then the line that generalises it:

> Anytime there's a should involved, it's situational awareness distortion.

A should is a rule carried into a situation from outside it, so it overwrites what is actually in front of you. That is the cost — not that the rule is wrong, but that holding it stops you seeing.

Dave's concrete version: sometimes you genuinely want to micromanage, and shoulding yourself out of it "creates all sorts of drama and mess, and your team can feel it." Stated plainly instead — *for this thing I want to dictate it* — "everyone will breathe a sigh of relief." → [[Did you tell them you want them to do it]]

He does not pretend it is free. Micromanage constantly and "you probably won't attract or retain people who desire autonomy" — which he presents as a real trade-off to choose, not a rule to obey. Jesse's test lands it: would you be hands-on selling the company? Always yes.
