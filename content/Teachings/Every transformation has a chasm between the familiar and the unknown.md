# Every transformation has a chasm between the familiar and the unknown

Dave names a structure he sees in nearly every transformational experience:

> There's this familiar way of being — and I know that one, I know what to do, I know it works. And then there's this new way of being, and there's this chasm between the two, because I don't know it. That's the unknown.

The familiar side's advantage is not that it's better. It's that it's *known to function*, however badly — "at least I know it quote-unquote works, to the degree that it does." Which is why founders keep a method with acknowledged toxic side effects, burnout included. A working bad option beats an unknown one under threat.

> Every time I or a client leaps into the unknown, it expands what's possible. Versus the familiar — if you always do what you always did, you always get what you always got.

Jesse's own crossing is the case study, and the useful detail is the dip in the middle. Ambush grew for five or six years on dirty fuel; then he started working with Dave and the business went flat for a while — "I was in neutral, I had this old way but there's this new way, and I don't really understand this new way." He eventually stepped down as CEO and started Gateway X, which created more value in two or three years than everything before it.

The flat period is the chasm. Anyone crossing should expect it. → The Edge Expansion Process
