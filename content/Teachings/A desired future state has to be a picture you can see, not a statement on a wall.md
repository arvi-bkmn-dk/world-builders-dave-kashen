# A desired future state has to be a picture you can see, not a statement on a wall

Dave draws a hard line between a DFS and the artefacts companies usually mistake for one.

> It's not just a paragraph somewhere, it's not a mission statement, it's not some words on the wall. It's an actual picture or movie in your mind's eye — or your team's mind's eye — of the future.

The reason is mechanical rather than aesthetic. The tension that does the work comes from the mind's inability to separate what it sees from what it envisions, which generates the cognitive dissonance the subconscious then goes to work resolving. A sentence does not produce that. An image does.

> Our minds can't really tell the difference between what we are seeing and what we are envisioning. Our subconscious mind goes to work to bridge the gap, and that's the source of creativity.

He demonstrates rather than describes it. Having got Jesse's vision stated as numbers and conditions, he does not stop there — he has him close his eyes, place himself at the end of 2025, and *inhabit* it: "stepping into the body of future Jesse, who's there having that experience. Just notice what is it like to be you?" What comes back is not the metrics but the felt quality — spacious, energised, grateful. That felt quality is the thing that pulls.

Jesse's separate complaint about specificity is the same point from the other side: vagueness fails because you cannot picture a vague thing. "How many clients, how many employees, what will the org chart look like?" — specificity is what makes the image renderable.
