# Every company has a co-founder who left and got erased from history

Gagan's observation, and its value is entirely in the normalising.

> Basically every company that I've been involved with, and almost every company I've ever heard of, has a co-founder departure. I cannot think of a company that I know of that doesn't have a co-founder who was really early, who eventually left. And then that person usually gets erased from history.

The erasure is why it stays invisible. The departures happen at roughly universal rates and leave no public trace, so each founder living through one believes they are the exception.

Dave names the cost of that silence:

> It really is not normalised. So it can feel like — oh my God, what's wrong with me, that I can't make this work with my co-founders.

His reframe: a co-founder relationship is "almost maybe more intense than a marriage," with extra layers of alignment on top — goals, pace, how you want to do business. "Given that 50% of marriages end in divorce, it's not shocking that co-founder marriages often do too."

Gagan widens it past co-founders to all churn, with numbers: of your first ten employees, "five years in, the chances that you have more than 20% of that team is pretty low." Maven is at 50% and he calls that lucky. "If you look at old offsite pictures, half the people are gone. It's normal."

Which matters in both directions — for the leader carrying guilt, and for the person leaving: "you might have been a perfect fit for year zero to two, and it's no longer a fit. It doesn't mean you're a bad human."
