# Denying a part of yourself makes you less behaviorally flexible

The business case for shadow work, stated as capability rather than wellbeing.

Dave's own shadow was the jerk: "shorthand for it would be — I am not an asshole. I'm a kind, loving, nurturing, supportive guy." So when he saw someone assertive or aggressive, the judgment came fast: "what a Neanderthal."

What changed when he owned it:

> I actually became a better coach, because I'm now able to challenge my clients and be like — hold on, that's not true what you just said. Or, that's bullshit.

And the general principle:

> I was denying a part of myself, so I was less whole and less behaviorally flexible. If you can only act in certain ways, then you can only create certain outcomes. But if you're dynamic, then you can create a wider variety of outcomes.

A denied part is a **removed option**. The situations that call for it get handled with something else, worse.

Dave's client example recurs from HoE 11 — the engineering-background founder whose struggling team needed a cheerleader, who said "that's not me, that's not authentic," and got: "bullshit. It is just not comfortable." → [[That is not authentic is usually just not comfortable]]

His framing of the obligation is worth keeping: "you're here to serve, and if what the situation calls for is this thing, then being able to access all those parts of yourself is key."
