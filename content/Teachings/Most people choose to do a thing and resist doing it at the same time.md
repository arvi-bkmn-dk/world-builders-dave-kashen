# Most people choose to do a thing and resist doing it at the same time

Andrew goes for a run and carries guilt the whole way. Dave's response isn't about whether the run was a good use of the hour.

> Most people choose to do the thing and resist doing the thing at the same time. Okay, you're choosing to run — go enjoy the run. Or you choose it, but then resist it, because in their mind you're judging: should I be doing this, I shouldn't be doing this.

The cost is paid twice. You spend the hour and you don't get the hour, because you were elsewhere for it. Nothing was gained by the resistance — the run happened either way.

Dave's willingness question lands the point cleanly: *are you willing to just run for 20 minutes and enjoy it, without giving yourself any kind of guilt of any kind?* → [[The Willingness Question]]

Jesse's picture of the alternative:

> If you could run, and you were running; and you could work, and you were working — and in neither of those two things are you doing the one and thinking about the other.

The same doubling shows up in Dave's taxes, where he did the work *and* dragged his feet and complained, making himself and his wife miserable for hours of a task that was fine once he chose it. → [[The same activity feels different from have-to than from get-to]]
