# Grieving the future you expected is what frees you to create one

An exercise Dave gave Jesse repeatedly, aimed at a version of yourself that never arrived.

> If there's a part of you who thought you should have been a billionaire by now, and it turns out you're not — write a letter to that billionaire guy inside of you, and let him go.

The letter Jesse describes is a farewell rather than a repudiation: *you were there when I was 20, you wanted this, you started the billion-dollar website idea, you did all these things. Thanks for everything you've done for me. Now I want to say goodbye. I'm going to grieve you. I'm going to mourn you.*

> It sounds kind of corny. Once you do it, it's so effective.

Dave's insistence on what makes it work:

> It's not just the intellectual exercise. It's that you actually feel the sadness, the grief.

An inventory of what didn't happen, written without grief, is just another self-assessment.

The crucial qualifier is that this is not about abandoning the ambition:

> If you still have that desire — awesome. Wishing you were already there when you're not is not helping you get there. That's the irony. It's actually in your way. "I thought I'd get there by this age, I'm not there. Okay — but in the next decade I want to become that." Imagine it, move toward it.

Which is exactly [[Frustration is a desired future collapsed into the present]]: the grief is what puts the future back in the future, where it can pull rather than accuse.
