# Love that does not arrive in the expected form does not get received

Dave's most personal story in his work, and the clearest case of [[Receiving is a bigger blind spot than giving]].

> For most of my life I felt like my dad didn't love me unconditionally. I couldn't really feel his love — it felt more like judgment. Until I finally one day realised: I wasn't letting it in. It didn't come in the exact form that I thought it should or wanted. Ultimately I just wasn't receiving it.

Asked what specifically he'd missed, the answer is stark. His father said *I love you.* Dave heard it and discounted it, because of everything around it:

> He gets off the phone quickly. He hasn't made an effort to come spend time with me. When I was a kid he didn't play with me, he didn't show emotions, he didn't listen — all the ways that my mind said *this is how love is supposed to look.*

The mind had a specification. Evidence that didn't match the specification was rejected, including the words themselves. Which is [[A story is the meaning your mind adds to neutral facts|the fact/story distinction]] operating on an entire relationship over decades.

What finally pierced it was a translation into terms Dave couldn't dispute, because he was already living them:

> You know how you feel about Ben and Brody, or my two boys — that's how I feel about you.

His two boys. Nothing about his father's behaviour changed — the form was the same as it had always been.

> That just cracked it for me. It opened up and I let it in and felt it, in some way, for the first time. It wasn't an idea — I *felt* his love.

He notes what the unreceived love had been costing: a story that he had to be successful, polite, meeting some ideal expectation in order to be loved, and driving himself to achieve in order to become worthy of it. → [[Core Beliefs]]
