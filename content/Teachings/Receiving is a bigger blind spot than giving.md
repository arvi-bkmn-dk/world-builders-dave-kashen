# Receiving is a bigger blind spot than giving

Dave names what he thinks is most people's largest blind spot, and it isn't stinginess.

> Most people are not open to receiving. And that may actually be the biggest blind spot for most people.

The evidence arrives live: Dave appreciates Andrew for his vulnerability, then asks him directly whether he let it in. Andrew is honest — no. He was in his head, annoyed that Dave hadn't acknowledged what he'd asked for.

Jesse's observation is the general form: these are "all just ways we try to avoid feeling love and letting it in."

What makes this a blind spot rather than a choice is that it doesn't feel like refusing. It feels like the appreciation was wrong, or mistimed, or aimed at the wrong thing. The deflection presents as a judgment about the gift rather than an unwillingness to receive.

Dave's own extreme case is [[Love that does not arrive in the expected form does not get received]] — decades of believing his father didn't love him unconditionally.

His insistence on scaling it down matters, because the dramatic version lets everyone off:

> I think we're doing this every day with all the people in our lives in tiny ways. I'm giving you the most extreme example for me. I really want people to reflect on all the love that's around you that you're not receiving.

Which reframes the problem entirely. The scarcity may not be in the supply.
