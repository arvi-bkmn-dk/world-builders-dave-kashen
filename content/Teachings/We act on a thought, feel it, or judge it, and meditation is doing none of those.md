# We act on a thought, feel it, or judge it, and meditation is doing none of those

Jesse's model of what normally happens when a thought arrives — three responses, and they cover almost everything.

1. **You act.** *Oh, I've got to send that email.*
2. **You feel something.** *Why did they say that to me? I'm mad about it.*
3. **You judge or argue with it.** *No, no, that's wrong.*

> Those are the three things we typically do with thoughts. And meditation is literally just the practice of having the thought and not reacting to it.

Setting out the three makes the practice specific rather than vague. "Don't react" is hard to operationalise; *don't act, don't get pulled into the feeling, don't argue with it* is a checklist.

Jesse's account of the benefit is unusually modest, and more credible for it:

> Things would happen that used to really throw me off my game. And just by not reacting for one more second, or five more seconds, it didn't feel like a big deal all of a sudden.

One to five seconds. That's the entire claim, and it's enough to change the response.

His framing of the mechanism is physical:

> A lot of that was just literally like the gym — getting my brain to go: thought is going to happen. Thought, thought, thought. Oh, there, that one goes, there, that one goes.

Reps on a bench rather than an insight — the same framing as Treat the startup as an emotional intensity gym. → [[The Meditation Practice]]
