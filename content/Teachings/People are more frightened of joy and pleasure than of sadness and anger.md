# People are more frightened of joy and pleasure than of sadness and anger

Counterintuitive and, Dave says, reliably observable in a room:

> I think people are even more scared of intimacy and joy and pleasure than they are of sadness, anger and fear.

His evidence is workshop behaviour. Give people an hour to feel sadness, anger or fear and they generally go with it — it's valuable, it's recognisable as work. Announce that the next hour is for feeling pleasure and joy and it freaks them out.

The trigger in the episode is Andrew's story of telling a well-dressed father at his kid's school *you are very handsome, I like the way you're dressed* — after which the man stopped talking to him for weeks. Dave isn't surprised: a vulnerable, warm, unsolicited approach is exactly what people have least capacity for.

This is [[The Upper Limit Problem]] appearing at the level of a single moment. The thermostat isn't only about how successful your life is allowed to get; it governs how much good feeling is permitted in an afternoon.

And it completes the capacity argument. Expanding your range isn't only about tolerating anger, fear and sadness — the pleasant end has a ceiling too, and it is often lower:

> It's not just expanding your capacity to be with anger, fear, sadness. It's also expanding your capacity to be with joy and pleasure and excitement.

Jesse supplies a live instance: avoiding looking at his own calendar because his setup at Gateway X is going so well that he feels guilty about it — "am I allowed to do all these things?"
