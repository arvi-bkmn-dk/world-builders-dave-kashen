# What got you here will not get you there

Dave's case that scaling is more about **unlearning** than learning — because the instincts that built the company are the ones now blocking it.

> Every founder starts out in survival mode. You had to do it all — close every deal, hire every team member, make every decision. Hustle, speed, and relentless execution were your superpowers. But as your company scales, those same instincts start working against you:
> * Your bias for speed turns into micromanaging and bottlenecking decisions.
> * Your need for control prevents your team from stepping up.
> * Your attachment to being the smartest problem-solver stops you from empowering others.

Each was genuinely a strength. That is what makes it hard — you are not being asked to fix a weakness but to retire something that worked.

The three things he says nearly every founder has to unlearn:

**1. More work equals more success.** "Delegate before you feel ready — you'll never feel ready. Prioritize leverage over effort. If you're working harder than your team, something is broken."

**2. The need to have all the answers.** "If your team relies on you for all the answers, you've built a fragile system. Replace giving answers with asking better questions." → [[Your job is not to have the right answers but to build a team that finds them without you]]

**3. Pressure equals performance.** The objection Dave hears — *if I stop stressing, won't I get complacent?* — and his answer: "high performance doesn't come from pressure. It comes from clarity, presence, and focus. Calm, focused leadership isn't weak — it's a competitive advantage."

The diagnostic question: **what do I need to unlearn?**

> The founders who struggle cling to old patterns, believing that what worked at 10 people will still work at 100.
