# Accepting things as they are maximizes the probability of them changing

The paradox stated directly:

> **Accepting things the way they are maximizes the probability of them evolving and changing and growing.**

Which is the exact opposite of what non-acceptance promises. Jesse asks the fair question — *why is non-acceptance such an effective form of motivation?* — and Dave grants that it works, mechanically:

> When our system experiences **tension**, it feels like **pain** in the system. We're wired to move toward pleasure and away from pain, so our system naturally wants to **resolve** that tension.

> And it's **so easy to create tension**, because our mind has a huge bias toward **what's wrong, what's missing, what could hurt us.** It seems to perceive that it's **way safer to believe there's something wrong** than to believe everything's okay.

So non-acceptance is the **default path** — cheap to generate, and genuinely motivating. The cost is what it costs:

> From my experience, it creates **suffering.** The Buddha talks about **resistance and attachment** as the two primary sources of human suffering. **Resistance is non-acceptance.**

> Your company is exactly as it is — it's not any different than it is, **by definition.** So not accepting it is just **resisting what is.**

And the physical bill: *"so many health issues are caused by stress… your boss has a bigger impact on your health than your doctor."* → [[Your manager has a greater impact on your health than your doctor]]

Dave's accounting of what non-acceptance actually buys, over a seven-year arc: years of reactive survival mode, difficulty being present with family, then an exit — and *"for a week you're rejoicing. A week, if you're lucky."* → [[The biggest feeling founders have at exit is relief]]

> Basically all you've been doing is **external work** — changing your external conditions without changing your internal conditions. And **if you don't change your internal conditions, your internal conditions don't change.**
