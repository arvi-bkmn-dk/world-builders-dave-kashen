# Busyness is the most respectable way to avoid a feeling

Dave lists the conscious routes out of a feeling — wine, a joint, scrolling, games — and then names the one that draws no suspicion:

> I have a friend who keeps his calendar really full.

> Most of man's struggles come from his inability to sit quietly alone in a room.

> I think the reason we tend to have such a hard time being still is because there's some feeling we've been avoiding feeling that, once we get still, starts to arise.

Which turns into a question about the population he works with:

> If you think about founders, who are some of the busiest people I know — it's interesting to wonder how much of their busyness is just avoiding some feeling they don't want to feel.

Jesse supplies a controlled experiment on himself. Having stepped back from operating the companies he chairs, his calendar emptied — and what arrived was anxiety and sadness together:

> I can almost feel what I've been running from for so long.

The value of his example is that it isolates the variable. At his first company the busyness had a watertight justification: "I'm the CEO, this is my first company, it's all my livelihood, I must stay busy in order for it to be successful." Now the same feeling persists where the justification cannot:

> It's just objectively false that I have to stay busy for these things to do well. But the feeling is just as present as it was.

Which exposes the belief underneath — "I'm not worthwhile if I'm not busy." Dave identifies the feeling gluing that in place as shame, and notes that dismantling the belief argumentatively will not work until the shame is felt. → [[Unfelt feelings are the glue that keeps unwanted patterns in place]]
