# A gratitude practice only gets you to neutral

Jesse's framing of gratitude, and its modesty is what makes it convincing.

> Our brains are designed for survival. We're designed to scan for threat. Most people I know, including me for many years, would wake up and the first thing I'd do is look at the dashboard, look at my email — what's wrong, what do I need to address? Because I want to stay alive.

The reality being scanned is usually nothing like the threat report:

> If you wake up — living in a nice house, sleeping next to a wonderful wife, you're going to eat in the morning…

Then the claim:

> If you don't take that time to intentionally show yourself what is good, you won't even be at neutral. A gratitude journal just gets you to neutral. Forget about feeling positive — it just curbs the natural tendency of your survival brain in the other direction.

Calling it a correction rather than an enhancement is what makes it credible. It isn't manufacturing positivity; it's counterweighting a bias that is already distorting the input. Which is the same argument as [[Your inner voice reports a dystopian version of a reality you are not in]] — the practice is for accuracy.

**The version Andrew prefers** is Jesse's Friday habit of sending gratitude to people directly, and both report the same arc: it feels weird and faking at first, then good, with a bounce-back when the other person responds. Andrew: *I feel douchey at first, but then I feel great.*

Jesse's reaction to that being the norm: "isn't it sad? It's kind of sad that we live in a world where that's weird." And the effect of scheduling it — "it generally leads to me just doing it more often without prompting myself to." → [[The Acknowledgement Party]]
