# When you blame yourself you are much less curious

Why self-blame feels like accountability and functions as its opposite.

> Sometimes the whole shame and blame thing is a way to avoid actually facing the costs — and taking responsibility. We'll often be like, okay, I blame myself, I shouldn't be late, this is bad to be late. And it's almost like: okay, I've slapped myself on the wrist, so now I'm off the hook.

The self-reproach closes the matter. It looks like taking it seriously and it ends the inquiry at the exact point the inquiry would start.

> Ironically, when you blame yourself, you're much less curious. And so you don't really face what's creating the pattern, and also what the impact of it is.

Two things go unexamined: the mechanism producing the behaviour, and its actual cost to other people. Both are only reachable from curiosity, and blame forecloses it.

This is the same move [[We slap ourselves on the wrist and let ourselves off the hook instead of taking the learning]] describes for regret, and the same structure as the hero who takes the blame in [[Heroes require victims]] — *well, I'm the leader, I take the blame* — which Dave elsewhere calls a way of not having to think about it anymore.

The alternative is the cost-benefit question, which requires you to be genuinely interested in why you do the thing. → [[What are the benefits of the behavior you want to change]]
