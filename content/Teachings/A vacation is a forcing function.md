# A vacation is a forcing function

Dave's client was drowning in detail — *"either micromanaging or heavily involved with everything, you could call it either one depending on your perspective"* — and then got married, with a non-negotiable two-week honeymoon.

> He was terrified. Because in his mind, taking two weeks away meant everything would fall apart.

They spent months preparing. *"He had to hire people. He had to let go of things. He had to shift his role from being in the weeds doing everything to a much higher level of leading and orchestrating."*

> The irony? **Preparing to step away made him more valuable to his company than any hour he'd spent buried in the work.**

The reason it has to be forced is that the conditions never clear on their own — Dave borrows Clayton Christensen's line from *How Will You Measure Your Life?*: **"life is a series of extenuating circumstances,"** and a startup especially so. → [[Life is a series of extenuating circumstances]]

> What that means is **it never feels like the right time.**

What the absence produces is diagnostic:

> If you're gone for a week, you really get to see what falls apart. You have to think about who you can delegate to, how to delegate well, what context to give people. **Or if there's no one you can delegate to, that tells you something about your hiring.**

And it is also nervous-system work, not only logistics:

> You may need to **show your nervous system** that things don't fall apart. I've seen a lot of founders find a lot of freedom when they come back after a week and find things are still running okay. People stepped up in surprising ways.

What happened: *"things didn't fall apart. His company didn't just survive. It was stronger… If not for that honeymoon, I think he'd still be micromanaging and in the weeds today."*
