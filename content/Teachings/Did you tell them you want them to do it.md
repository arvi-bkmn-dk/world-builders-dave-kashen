# Did you tell them you want them to do it

The simplest leadership move in his work, and Dave reports it working embarrassingly often.

> I have so many clients like — I don't know how to get my team to do blah blah blah. And I'm like: did you tell them you want them to do blah blah blah? And they're like, oh no.

What was happening instead:

> They're trying to figure out the right answer of how to manipulate them. It's like — just tell them you want it. And it's amazing how many times, that's just as simple as that.

The word doing the work is **want**. A should is impersonal and arguable — *we need to, we have to, this is what companies do.* A want is a person stating a position, which is both harder to say and harder to argue with. → Speaking Unarguably

Dave sets this inside the wider *want over should* distinction: most of us run on should most of the time, and his response to "what should I do?" is "according to whom?"

He goes further and treats wants as the most reliable signal available about a person: "I trust people's wants more than I think anything else in terms of what's authentic for them." Jesse's worked example is Heather at Growth Assistant — he had been "banging the table" for an AI-agents strategy for months, and reversed entirely when the person responsible turned out to want something adjacent and was genuinely excited about it.
