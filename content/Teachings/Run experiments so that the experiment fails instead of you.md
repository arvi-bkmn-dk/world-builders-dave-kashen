# Run experiments so that the experiment fails instead of you

Dave, crediting Joe Hudson, identifies why a single failed attempt so often closes a category permanently.

> There's a part of our brain that's responsible for us not failing. Think about a lion that tries to become the dominant lion and gets beaten down — don't do that again, you're going to get killed.

So coding an outcome as *failure* doesn't merely discourage; it recruits protective machinery against retrying. The mechanism is doing its job, which is why willpower doesn't override it.

The workaround is grammatical, and that's not a trick — it changes what gets coded:

> The experiments either fail or don't fail. It's not "I'm a failure." It's: oh, that experiment failed. Next.

A failed experiment is information about a configuration. A failure is information about you, and only the second triggers the lockout.

Andrew's admission shows the lockout in place:

> I had a block in my brain that told me there's certain things I just can't do. These are things I've accepted that I'm not good at, and I've shifted my attention away from them.

One attempt at getting his phone away from his bed hadn't worked, and the conclusion had generalised to a permanent trait. Dave's advice — try a few other options — is almost insultingly simple, and Andrew is honest that it took relationship leverage before he could receive it.
