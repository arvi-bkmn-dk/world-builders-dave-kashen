# Talent is not the defining factor of your success in a high variance business

Gagan's case, made from having met founders at every tier of outcome:

> The talent level, at a certain point, is not the defining factor. And your decision making is not the defining factor of your success — especially in such a high variance business.

His test is deliberately extreme: was Jeff Bezos, starting Amazon, *two times* smarter than someone who started a company that didn't work?

> I wouldn't even be surprised if the variance there is so low that it's imperceptible. Jeff Bezos could have been 20% dumber and still had the same outcome, and you wouldn't be able to predict it.

He is careful about what this does and doesn't mean. Brian Chesky today has learned things Gagan hasn't — "his experiences are different, and that's hard to compete with." The claim is about the *starting* distribution, not the accumulated one: at the point of founding, among the thousands attempting the same thing, talent doesn't separate the outcomes.

The evidence from the other side is VC hit rates. Even the best "bat like a baseball player at 300," and a typical fund killing it is at 10%. If the professionals whose job is predicting this are right one time in ten, the outcome is not primarily a talent readout.

Gagan's conclusion is personal rather than fatalistic:

> I am an N of one. I make decisions that fit me really well. And I cannot assume that that is going to lead to X, Y or Z levels of success compared to others. But I can assume that because I'm sufficiently talented and doing my best, I'll get something that will be sufficient for me.

Which is the ground under [[You make it mean so much more about you than it actually means]].
