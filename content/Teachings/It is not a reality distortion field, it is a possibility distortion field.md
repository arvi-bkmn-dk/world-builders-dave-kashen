# It is not a reality distortion field, it is a possibility distortion field

Andrew's challenge is the best objection in the episode: isn't being an entrepreneur *precisely* refusing to accept reality — visualising what you want, ignoring what you don't, and working until the world matches?

Dave's answer separates two things the phrase "reality distortion field" runs together:

> What Steve Jobs was very gifted at, and a lot of entrepreneurs are gifted at, is distorting people's sense of **what is possible in the future**. When I say accept reality, what I mean is what's actually already true — the past and the present. You can bend the future.

Jesse renames it and Dave immediately adopts it:

> It's not really a reality distortion field. They should call it a **possibility distortion field.**

And the ignoring half gets rejected outright: "if you're ignoring what's actually true, that is not a winning strategy." Jesse's counter-example is Apple, supposedly the home of the reality distortion field, and relentless about killing its own products — "very gifted at both DFS and current reality."

Jesse's land-and-house metaphor carries it: you can hold a vivid vision of the house *and* survey the ground carefully. Not accepting reality is standing on the plot thinking *why is this still just land, why isn't the house here yet* — spending creative energy wishing you were already in the desired future.

**Where it meets the shadow.** Dave closes the loop: what stops founders declaring extraordinary possibilities is "that fear of being a fraud, or being a failure, or being a liar." A founder pitching a billion-dollar outcome is not lying — "they know what game we're playing" — but an unowned fraud shadow will block a normal, healthy leadership act.
