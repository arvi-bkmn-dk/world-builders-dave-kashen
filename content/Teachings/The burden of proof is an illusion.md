# The burden of proof is an illusion

Dave asks Gagan what his burden of proof is now, gets *"I don't know — is there a burden of proof?"*, and admits the setup:

> I think it's an illusion. That was a leading question, I was hoping you'd say that. I don't think there is any burden of proof. And proving what, to whom? Like, what do we exactly mean by that?

The two questions do the work. A burden of proof requires a **claim** and an **audience**, and under examination neither survives. The thing to be proven is usually *that I am good* or *great* — not a proposition evidence could settle. And the audience is generally absent, historical, or invented.

Gagan's history shows the mechanism. His burden rose *with* his success — not the shape of something being discharged. Underneath it: "feeling like I had to prove to somebody that I was good," traced to childhood signals that he was "good, but not great."

And what dissolved it was not more proof. It was three years with none of it — "completely free in the world, financially secure, doing whatever I want" — and finding "I was so happy doing nothing, that I could not possibly be solely defined by the burden of proof."

He is honest that it is not gone: he still compares himself to friends running billion-dollar companies, "and that is a chip on my shoulder a little bit." What changed is that it stopped being the organising principle.

Dave's replacement premise: "we're inherently and born worthy, and the rest is just what we choose to do."
