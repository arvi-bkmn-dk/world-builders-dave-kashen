# Anytime there is something we want but are not getting, there is a part of us that does not want it

A line Dave says he uses with clients often, and it turns an apparent failure of execution into a parts conflict.

> Anytime there's something we want but aren't getting, there's a part of us that doesn't want it.

Which relocates the question. Not *why can't I achieve this* — a question that invites more effort — but *what in me is against it*, which is answerable.

He grounds it in the parts model, quoting the book he is writing:

> Most of us have had the experience of wanting to eat that next piece of chocolate cake and not wanting to eat it at the same time. How is that possible?

> We are made up of multiple "parts." Different aspects of ourselves hold different perspectives, fears, desires, and beliefs. When these perspectives clash, we experience **parts conflicts.**

Money is where he sees it most, and the conflicting parts are all sincere:

> One part desperately wants financial success while another views wealth with suspicion or even moral judgment. Many founders reveal a clingy, fearful part that holds tightly to every dollar because it never knows when the next one is coming. Simultaneously, they might have a rebellious part that rejects money altogether. Some even discover a part that fears success itself — worried that if they actually achieve wealth, they'll become the very type of person they've always judged negatively.

And the cost is operational rather than merely internal:

> This constant tension depletes your creative energy, clouds your judgment, and ultimately hinders your company's growth. When our internal parts are at war, our actions often reflect that fragmentation. Leadership decisions feel inconsistent, reactive, or charged with unspoken fear.

→ [[Both parts want the same outcome and have different strategies]] is the resolution.
