# Healthy expression is the third option between reacting and holding it in

Dave names the false binary that keeps anger stuck, using the emotion where it bites hardest:

> People think I can either be violent and reactive and uncivilised and cause pain and damage, or I can just hold it in.

Both options are bad, so people pick holding it in and pay for it continuously. The third option is to express the energy deliberately, somewhere safe.

The mechanism is that emotion is energy that wants a physical route out — "there's a sound and movement that the energy tends to want to be expressed as." This is why reactive anger produces yelling and thrown objects: the form is correct, the target is not. Conscious leadership's version is matching inner energy with outer expression, typically through **sound and movement**.

Dave's own practice, from Diana Chapman:

> I now have a whiffle ball bat in my bedroom. And I'll just go and, while screaming — 'cause movement and sound — I'll be like, ahh, and I'll bash my pillow. It's safe. I'm not hurting the pillow, I'm not hurting myself, I'm not hurting anyone.

He tells his family in advance or does it when nobody is home. Jesse's equivalent is screaming in the car.

Different emotions want different movements. Fear wants shaking — "like zebras do after they're done fleeing the lion," literally shaking hips and hands, which Dave describes as "off-gassing the fear." Sadness may want the floor and a fetal position. Anger wants something bounded and percussive. Running moves fear more than anger.

Two cautions. **Exercise cuts both ways** — "people can either use it to feel and express, or to distract and avoid," and Dave admits to having used it for the latter for years. And **most people have never done this at all**: "most people, their entire lives, have never spent 2, 3, 4, 5 minutes expressing a feeling of anger."

His suggested entry point is anger, because "it tends to be the outer shell" — often protecting sadness or something more vulnerable — and because it is the least vulnerable to feel.
