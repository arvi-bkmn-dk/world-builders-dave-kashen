# Make a list of your team members and find something to appreciate each week

The practice, stated as a routine because Dave's whole argument is that appreciation does not happen without one.

> * Make a list of each of your team members
> * Each week, look for something to appreciate them for and write it down next to their name. Either some way that they **are** (being) or something that they **did** (doing)
> * Find time to deliver the appreciations. Look them in the eyes and tell them "I appreciate you for…" (or if you won't have a chance, at least shoot them a text or email)

Three details carry it. The **list** forces coverage, so appreciation doesn't concentrate on whoever is most visible. The **being/doing split** keeps it from collapsing into performance review — → [[Appreciate who someone is, not only what they did]]. And **looking them in the eyes** is the part people skip, because delivery is where the discomfort lives.

The case for bothering, from Dave's clients:

> Appreciation is more valuable to people than thousands, even tens of thousands of dollars in compensation and perks. I've had clients tell me about a single moment of appreciation they received from a manager — years, even a decade ago — that they still remember vividly.

> That's how rare and powerful genuine appreciation is.

A decade-long memory from one sentence is a strange return on effort, and the rarity is what produces it.

Worth noting the other side, which the retreat exposed: receiving is harder than giving. Five hours of appreciation left founders squirming. → [[Receiving is a bigger blind spot than giving]]
