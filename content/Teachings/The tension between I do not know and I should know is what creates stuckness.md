# The tension between I do not know and I should know is what creates stuckness

> One of the most common patterns that founders call feeling stuck is: **I don't know how to do something, and I think I should know how to do it.**

> For most founders, a lot of the things they're doing, it's the **first time they're ever doing it.** So at some level it's **absurd** that they think they should already know all the answers. But they do.

The reason they do is the same conditioning as [[In school they taught us the material before the test]]:

> Many have gotten a lot of accolades and achievement by knowing the right answers. So when they encounter a situation where they don't know, at least the voice in their head says: *you should know how to do this.*

> That's part of what creates that feeling of stuckness — this **tension between I don't know and I should know.**

Dave's first move is to remove one side of the tension rather than resolve it:

> Often my first move is: **well, can you just be with "I don't know"?**

> If your cup is already full, there's no room for new knowledge, new learning.

And the practical consequence, which reverses the intuition that certainty enables action:

> Ironically, the thought *I should know how to do this* actually **gets in the way of action.** You just get stuck around *I should know, but I don't know.* Versus **"God, I don't know"** — it invites a state of **curiosity and wonder**, from which ideas emerge to try and experiment.

Jesse's own version: *"I love 'I don't know' because I find it really liberating… it galvanizes action."* And Rick Elias, after buying a billion-dollar public company, saying *"we have no clue what we're doing"* — which Jesse first read as false modesty and then understood: *"he was trying to create room for himself. He was talking to himself."*
