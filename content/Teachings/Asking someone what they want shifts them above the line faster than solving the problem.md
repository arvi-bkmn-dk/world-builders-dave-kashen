# Asking someone what they want shifts them above the line faster than solving the problem

The most portable move in the episode, and Dave frames it as almost trivially simple.

> Often your team members come to you with problems — this is like the air we breathe as leaders. So the other place you can use DFS is you just go: okay, hold on. What's your desired future state? What would you like to have happen if this were all resolved to your complete delight and satisfaction?

What it does is change the person's state, not the problem's difficulty:

> It actually creates a state shift in them. They go essentially from below the line to above the line — when they go from focusing on the problem, they're essentially doing this lack and fear thing. It's a really simple almost hack.

The result Dave reports is that the answer usually arrives on its own: "they can usually see how to get there when they felt totally stuck." The stuckness was a property of the state, not the situation. Somebody below the line is running [[Interpreting a situation as a problem is what drops you into blame|problem-and-blame]] machinery that narrows what they can see; the question moves them out of it, and their own capability comes back online.

There is a compounding benefit he names last, and it is the reason to prefer this over answering: "that way your team learns to solve their own issues more and more." Solving it for them resolves one instance. Asking it teaches the move.

Jesse's field version is blunter and works when things are hot — "so what do you want? What would you want right now? What can I do right now for you?"
