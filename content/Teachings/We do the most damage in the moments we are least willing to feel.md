# We do the most damage in the moments we are least willing to feel

Dave's argument for why this is urgent operationally rather than merely healthy.

> Most of the time we can kind of get away with operating relatively unconsciously. But those moments where the shit hits the fan, where things don't go the way we expect, where something intense is happening — that's usually where we cause the most damage. Trust takes a lifetime to build and a minute to destroy.

The asymmetry is the point. Unconsciousness is cheap on ordinary days and ruinous on the few that matter, and those are exactly the days when an intense feeling is present and unattended.

The mechanism:

> The times where we react like that is usually because there's some intense feeling in our body that we either don't notice, don't pay attention to, or don't want to feel. And the way we react to avoid feeling it is: we express it — but instead of doing it consciously, we just throw the stapler across the room or rip into somebody.

The energy is going to move. The only variable is whether it moves deliberately — screaming in your car, a wiffle-ball bat on the bed, which is Dave's actual suggestion — or leaks out at whoever is nearest.

> It's like, ultimately the difference between reacting and responding.

Jesse's version of the leak is worth keeping because it wasn't shouting. He'd ask *how come we didn't think to do this? Why are they doing it this way and we're not?* As Andrew observes, that's worse in a sense — it preys on people's existing insecurities without ever raising a voice. → [[Saying I feel fear was more effective than yelling at people]]
