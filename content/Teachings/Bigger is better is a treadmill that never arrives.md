# Bigger is better is a treadmill that never arrives

Andrew states the belief plainly and refuses to give it up: *bigger has got to be better.* Dave's objection is structural rather than moral.

> It's a treadmill that's always speeding up. More is better — well, then when do you have enough? When is it big enough? Never.

A comparative with no terminal value cannot be satisfied, so the destination it promises is unreachable by construction. Dave's evidence is the people who should have arrived: "there's lots of billionaires out there who are like, God, but my buddy Larry has 100 billion, Elon has 320 billion — what's my measly 1 billion?"

The self-referential trap is visible in Andrew's own answer when Dave asks what he's scared would happen if he let the belief go: that he'd accept a smaller life. Dave names the circularity — *I believe bigger is better, and I'm scared that if I let go of that belief my life won't be as big.* The belief supplies both the goal and the fear of losing the goal.

Dave's distinction is not between wanting a big business and not wanting one:

> There's nothing wrong with wanting to build a big business, or even visualizing a big business. It's: are you doing it from this place of there's something wrong with the way it is now — in which case you're not present. And "bigger is better" inherently implies it's not big enough yet.
