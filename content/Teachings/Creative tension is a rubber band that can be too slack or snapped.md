# Creative tension is a rubber band that can be too slack or snapped

The gap between current reality and the desired future state behaves like a rubber band, and Dave names both ways it stops producing force.

**Too slack.** The target is close enough that nobody is moved. "We're at five, we're gonna get to six, and people are like, okay." Leaders then misread this as a people problem — "why are my people not working that hard?" — when the diagnosis is structural: there is not enough tension in the system. He adds a second slackness cause that is easy to miss: no deadline. "We're gonna get to 10 someday in the future" generates nothing, where "a year from now" does.

**Snapped.** Set the target far enough beyond belief and the band breaks. "If you said, well, you're at five now, how about a hundred next year? At some point it just becomes magical thinking." Once it snaps there is no tension at all — the team is not stretched, it is disengaged.

Jesse names the cost of the snapped case, which is worse than neutral: the goal becomes "a joke, a laughing stock," or actively demoralising, because people "feel like they're losing every day, even though the business may be double — but you set the target at quadruple." A team can outperform badly and still experience it as failure.

Dave's calibration tool is explicit probability. Google's OKRs targeted roughly two-thirds odds; other teams run at 50% or 90%. He declines to name a right answer and points instead at self-knowledge: "how much risk is enlivening for us, and how much is actually demotivating." The target has to stay both genuinely wanted and inside the realm of believable.
