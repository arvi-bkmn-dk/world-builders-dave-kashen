# Personality is a defence structure built from the feelings you would not feel

The episode's most destabilising claim, and Jesse flags it as one of his biggest learnings — that personality is not a fixed given.

Dave describes the construction as a childhood process of narrowing:

> Especially in childhood, like the first eight years as I understand it, we sort of learn to constrict and constrict and constrict and bound ourselves and protect ourselves from unwanted experiences. If you cried and one of your parents is like, don't cry — okay, crying's bad, don't do that, I get negative attention when I do that.

Each such lesson removes a feeling from the available range. What remains — the particular set you will still allow — hardens into what gets called character:

> Those sets of emotions that you're willing to feel and not willing to feel become your personality.

Dave's phrase for the result is the sharp one:

> All these walls that we put up metaphorically to protect us become our prison when we become adults.

He is explicit that this is physical, not merely psychological: the constriction "lives in the fascia, it lives in our body," and he suspects it is behind a good deal of chronic stress and health trouble. It also means the blunting is real rather than a matter of inattention — "for a lot of people, they actually don't feel it. We've blunted our instrument."

Neither of them claims all personality is defensive. Jesse allows that "people have natural energetic tendencies," and notes the Enneagram's own position that you are all nine numbers — the type is where weight accumulated, not the whole of what is there.

The consequence is that what feels like immutable identity is substantially a record of old protection. Dismantling it is possible, and Jesse is honest about the cost: "it is a very jarring experience to start to break that down."
