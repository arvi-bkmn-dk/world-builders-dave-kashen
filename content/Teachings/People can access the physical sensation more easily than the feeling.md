# People can access the physical sensation more easily than the feeling

For people who cannot name what they are feeling, Dave starts one level lower — with raw sensation, which is more accessible than the label:

> I find it useful to look at the physical sensations, because it is a physical experience. Sometimes people can access that more easily than even the feeling.

Then a rough map, from conscious leadership, converts location into emotion:

| Where | Likely |
|---|---|
| Neck and shoulders | anger |
| Stomach constriction | fear |
| Chest constriction, pressure behind the eyes | sadness |
| Throat, jaw | the last line of defence — holding it back |

Dave singles out the jaw: "the last line of defence is our jaw — don't say that, don't speak it, don't cry out," which he offers as an explanation for chronic jaw pain and teeth grinding.

He runs it live. Jesse reports "a little tightness in my chest, like a little ball there. My shoulders feel a little pulled on." Dave reads it back through the map — "likely some sadness and some anger" — and moves straight to the next question: are you welcoming them, or bracing?

Jesse identifies the prerequisite before any of this works, which Dave endorses: slowing down enough to check at all. Their older practice was the **unarguable check-in** — thought, feeling, sensation — and Dave notes that most people live permanently in the first third. "If you're constantly in your head focusing on your thoughts, you're going to ignore and resist feelings."

He also flags a general prior worth adopting: "let's just assume any feeling you have, you're at least partially resisting."
