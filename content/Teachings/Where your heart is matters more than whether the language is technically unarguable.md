# Where your heart is matters more than whether the language is technically unarguable

Andrew's challenge to the whole practice is the right one: isn't this just manufacturing language so you can dance around what you actually want to say? Both answers concede the point and then locate the real variable somewhere else.

Jesse's version: the language doesn't work if it gets weaponised or if your heart isn't truly there. You can say all the correct words while below the line and furious — "you could say, and, and you're an asshole." The inverse also holds: if your heart is in the right place you can say *should* and it isn't the end of the world.

Dave's version is sharper because he shows the failure inside the correct form:

> Even speaking unarguably, I could say "the story I make up is you're an asshole and you don't care about me." It's obvious that I'm attached to being right about that story, even though mechanically I'm naming that it's a story. So it's the attachment to being right that's the essence, rather than the mechanics of the language.

Which lands on the underlying variable: attachment to being right, which is what showing up in a state of threat produces. The language is downstream. This is why the practice can't be evaluated on compliance — a perfectly formed unarguable sentence delivered from below the line is still an attack, and a clumsy *should* delivered from presence usually isn't.
