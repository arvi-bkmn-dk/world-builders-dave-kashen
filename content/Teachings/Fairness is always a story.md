# Fairness is always a story

What Dave says derails most negotiations before they start.

> **Fairness is always a story.** And when two people argue about whose story is more "fair," you just get two people trying to be right instead of two people trying to create a workable resolution.

Fairness feels like a fact — an objective standard both parties should be able to see — which is exactly why it produces deadlock. Two people each holding a self-evident truth have nothing to negotiate; they have a dispute about reality.

His move with clients is deliberately provocative. When someone brings him a conflict, instead of helping:

> I just say these two words: **"Prove it."**

> Usually dumbfounded, they'll respond confused: "Prove… prove it's not fair?" "Yes," I'll say. "Prove it."

> They scramble for justification — the numbers, the work they've done, the value their partner hadn't created. But as we dig deeper, something becomes clear: **their justifications are the exact thing preventing them from finding a solution and getting what they want.**

> When you're focused on justifying why you're right, you can't uncover the real underlying issues.

Dave credits Harvard's interest-based negotiation — positions versus interests — and then names what it leaves out:

> Before you can get curious about **their** interests, you have to get curious about **your own stories.** Because when you're operating from threat — convinced someone doesn't respect you or is trying to screw you over — you literally cannot access the curiosity required for real negotiation.
