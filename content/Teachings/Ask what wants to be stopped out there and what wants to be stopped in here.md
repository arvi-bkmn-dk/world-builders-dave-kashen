# Ask what wants to be stopped out there and what wants to be stopped in here

The wisdom question for anger is *what wants to be stopped?* — and Dave runs it in both directions, because the mind only ever volunteers the first.

> I find it useful to direct that question **both outward and inward.** And usually I can find value in both.

His own example is his kids and screen time:

> First I'm like — **what wants to be stopped out there?** *I wanna stop them being on screens so much, I think it's not healthy.*

> But I also ask: **and what wants to be stopped in here?** I'll **always** find something.

> In this case what I got was: *oh yeah — my desire to connect with them, or really **my fear of disconnection** from them, is in the way of me actually setting boundaries.*

Note what does and doesn't change:

> **The action was the same.**

The outward answer was correct. The inward answer explained why he hadn't already acted on it — which is the difference between knowing what to do and being able to do it. Skipping the second question leaves the obstacle in place and makes the same anger recur.

Dave frames the whole move as an application of responsibility rather than self-criticism — *"it's a version of taking a hundred percent responsibility"* — and of the emotion as benign: *"love is trying to alert me that there's some pattern that is not actually serving you, that wants to be stopped."*
