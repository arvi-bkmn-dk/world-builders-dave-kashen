# People hide behind authenticity when they mean comfort

Andrew says he can't imagine introducing an exercise like this without feeling awkward. Dave has heard the objection in its more respectable form many times — *that's just not authentic for me* — and doesn't accept it.

> A lot of clients will say, well, that doesn't feel authentic. Or I'll say, the team really needs you to be a cheerleader right now, and a technical engineer founder says: but that's not authentic for me. And I'm like — well, shit, it's just not *comfortable*.

The distinction he draws:

> There's an interesting distinction between who I've always been and what feels safe and familiar, versus being your full whole self and having the behavioural flexibility to try new ways of being.

Authenticity, as usually deployed, describes the habitual self and dresses up its boundaries as integrity. Which makes it uniquely effective as an excuse — it sounds like a principled refusal rather than an avoidance, so it doesn't invite examination.

> It's the biggest cliché in the world: if we just do what's comfortable, we don't grow. By definition growth is what happens outside your comfort zone.

Dave's alternative isn't to override the discomfort but to name it accurately:

> It's awkward — and that doesn't mean you shouldn't do it. It's: oh, it feels awkward. You feel scared, you feel worried.

Which is The Edge Expansion Process applied to intimacy rather than to a hard conversation or a risk. The claim that this is the *most* uncomfortable edge follows: → [[People are more frightened of joy and pleasure than of sadness and anger]]
