# Capacity to be with uncertainty matters more than getting better at prediction

Dave grants the case for prediction before dismantling its priority: *"if you make accurate predictions, you will make more money than if you make inaccurate predictions."* But:

> That might be a **local maximum** — because it's possible there was some other **step function** you could have created, if you had been less focused on being right and more in a state of curiosity and wonder.

Then he states the trade plainly:

> If I had to choose between **more certainty, better prediction of what's gonna happen** — and **more resilience and capacity to be with uncertainty and change** — I'm voting for **option two.**

And the reason it is the better investment:

> **If you can trust your future self to handle whatever actually occurs, then you don't need to predict as much.** It's somewhat of a defense mechanism. If you could trust your future self to handle your future problems, you wouldn't need to do as much planning and prediction. **You could be more in flow.**

The counter-evidence he reaches for is Long-Term Capital Management — *"some of the most brilliant predictors of the future ever in humanity. And they were right, and they were right, and they were right, until there was something they missed."* Plus the ordinary version: *"who knew COVID was gonna come?"*

His account of why we do it anyway:

> It's kind of **absurd how certain we feel.** We have this core need for certainty… somehow we feel **safer** when we believe we can predict what's gonna happen.
