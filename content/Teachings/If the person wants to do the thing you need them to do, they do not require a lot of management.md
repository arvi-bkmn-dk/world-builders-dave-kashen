# If the person wants to do the thing you need them to do, they do not require a lot of management

*Enjoy over manage* applied to hiring, and Dave reports it as Joe Hudson's actual practice:

> When Joe and Tara and the team interview, they basically say to somebody: what's your dream job? And if their dream job is the job, they hire them. And if it's not, they don't. Because if the person actually wants to do the thing that you need them to do, they don't require a lot of management.

Management load is treated as a **hiring output**, not a management skill. The question is asked before the offer, because afterwards it is too late.

Dave's clients confirm the pattern without acting on it: "my best employees — yeah, they don't require much. But I spend a lot of time pushing and driving." His follow-up is the useful one: *do you want to be doing that?*

Then he names what keeps the wrong person in the seat, and it is emotional rather than analytical:

> How much of that is just fear of letting people go, and tolerating people who aren't really a great fit out of taking more than a hundred percent responsibility — oh, I feel bad, they're married, or they have a kid.

Which connects it to the sadness problem: a leader who cannot feel the grief of ending things keeps them running. "I see a lot of clients who are managing over enjoying — and one of the ways that occurs is by keeping people who aren't really a fit."
