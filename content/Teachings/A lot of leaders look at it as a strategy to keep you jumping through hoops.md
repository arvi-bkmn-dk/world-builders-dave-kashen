# A lot of leaders look at it as a strategy to keep you jumping through hoops

Jesse pushes past the idea that leaders simply find appreciation uncomfortable.

> There are a lot of leaders out there who actually look at it as a strategy. I'm going to keep you jumping through hoops. I'm never going to let you know that you're doing it.

Then turns it on Andrew:

> You do that to yourself. I see you do it all the time: I'm never going to let myself feel like what I'm doing is good enough, because that's the thing that's going to keep me going.

Which is the same machinery as [[The startup is a growth dojo but most founders run it as a whipping room]] — dirty fuel applied to other people, or to yourself, on purpose.

Andrew asks the fair question: so what's the problem with that? The answers avoid arguing on principle.

**It's worse to work with, and worse to be.** Andrew's own observation: "it's not a fun way to work with someone who's constantly in *ah, this sucks* mode. When I'm on fire and everything feels great, I bring out more of my charisma, and then other people want to be around that."

**It doesn't switch off when you arrive.** Jesse's version is the one with teeth:

> Someone acts like that for 10 years thinking that once they make money their life's going to be great. They sell their company, they make money, and they're like: oh my God, I'm grossly unhappy. Because my entire life I've forcibly kept myself unhappy because I thought that's what the formula was. Nobody wakes up and just starts being happy after forcing themselves to be unhappy forever.

Jesse's concession is worth keeping, because it isn't a sales pitch: "if you're fine with how it goes, then don't worry about it, do your thing." The argument only applies if you'd rather not live that way. → [[Do-have-be is backwards and it never arrives]]
