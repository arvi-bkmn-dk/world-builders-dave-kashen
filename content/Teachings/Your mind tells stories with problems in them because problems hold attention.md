# Your mind tells stories with problems in them because problems hold attention

An unusually mechanical explanation of the negativity bias, and one that answers a question most treatments skip: why are the mind's stories so reliably *bad*?

Dave's answer is that the mind is a storyteller in the literary sense, and good stories require tension, conflict and drama. A mind competing for your attention will therefore manufacture problems — because a problem is engaging, and because you will stay and work on solving it.

> Your mind is a compelling storyteller — it will tell you stories with problems in them to hook your attention, and so you will engage with it in working to solve them. The mind wants to hook your attention and remain relevant.

The reframe is useful precisely because it is unflattering to the content. If the stories are negative *by construction* rather than by accurate observation, then their negativity carries no evidential weight at all. A catastrophic prediction isn't a signal that things are bad; it's a signal that the narrator is doing its job.

This also explains the durability of [[The Seven Storyteller Archetypes]] — each one is a reliable generator of a particular kind of tension, which is why they recur rather than resolve.

The interview version adds why the ego likes it: solving problems makes it feel useful and dominant.

> our ego minds are problem solving machines.
