# Pursuing what you actually want makes you most powerful and most vulnerable at once

Dave's answer for why people who know what they want still pursue something else.

> When we pursue the thing we really want and care deeply about, we're both most powerful and most vulnerable.

The two rise together, and that is the trap. The caring is what supplies the energy, and the caring is what makes failure unbearable — "it's heartbreaking to not get what you really want." Against that, a goal you merely should want is emotionally cheap to miss: "eh, okay, this thing, I might make money, it might not."

So chasing the should-goal is not a failure of self-knowledge. It is protective, and Dave names the exact shape of the protection:

> Not going after it fully almost gives you the excuse of, well, okay, but I didn't really try. You avoid some responsibility — but of course the cost of that is you're less likely to create what you really want.

Withholding full effort preserves an explanation for failure. The explanation costs you the outcome.

Jesse spots the same structure in venture-speak, where the impersonal framing does the insulating: "this is what companies should do, it's gonna get to a billion or not, and then if it fails, there's no big..." The reason that framing is comfortable is that it is not vulnerable. It was never what you wanted.

Dave's reframe is that going after the real thing is the courageous act, not the reckless one: "it takes real courage, because you care."
