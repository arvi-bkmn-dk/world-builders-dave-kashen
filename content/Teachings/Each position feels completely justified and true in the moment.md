# Each position feels completely justified and true in the moment

Why the drama triangle is so hard to catch from inside it — and Dave's answer is that every corner comes with its own conviction.

> In the span of a single meeting, a founder might feel victimized by market conditions, blame their team for inadequate execution, and then heroically announce they'll personally solve everything. **Each position feels completely justified and true in the moment.**

His client, after a missed product deadline, in one session:

> "We'll never hit our revenue targets now." *(Victim)*
> "The engineering team doesn't understand urgency." *(Villain)*
> "I need to step in and manage the team myself." *(Hero)*

> Each statement felt true to him. And kept him trapped in the same cycle.

None of the three is obviously irrational. That is the trap — the cycle is made of individually defensible sentences, so nothing in it triggers suspicion.

Dave then runs it on himself, which is the most useful part. After announcing a founder workshop in San Francisco to lower sign-ups than projected, he watched his own mind cycle in minutes:

> "Poor me, it's so hard to get people to sign up for group experiences." *(Victim)*
> "Don't they get what an incredible value this is? They'd have to pay 50x as much for these hours one-on-one." *(Villain)*
> "Why do I even bother with these group offerings? I should just stick to one-on-one coaching." *(Hero)*

> The power of these thoughts was striking — each felt completely justified and true in the moment.

A coach who teaches this framework, caught by it, and the tell was not that the thoughts felt wrong. They felt right.
