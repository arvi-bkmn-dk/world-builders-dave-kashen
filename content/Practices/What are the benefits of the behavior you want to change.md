# What are the benefits of the behavior you want to change

Dave's opening move when a client wants to change something about themselves — and it is deliberately the opposite of what they came for.

Jesse describes bringing him a habit and expecting a fix: "my natural inclination was, okay, give me the tools, fix me Dave." What he got instead was **what are the benefits of talking as much as you do, and what's it costing you?**

The reaction is reliable: "when you ask someone that, when they're telling you that they don't like something about themselves — the reaction is like, *what do you mean the benefits of it?*"

And the answers arrive quickly. Being unclear in your instructions "kept me safe, I didn't have to have a difficult conversation." Being late buys "the sense of freedom, not being a slave to my former commitments."

Why it works, in Dave's words:

> It implies what I think is true — that we are good. We might have ineffective strategies to get our needs met, but everything we do has intended positive outcome for us, given our perception of reality.

> The benefits question is an invitation to self-acceptance. It's very hard to sustainably shift what you don't accept.

Then, and only then, the second half: **what are the costs?** Which Dave says people avoid facing, "because if we did, we might actually shift them."

Jesse adds a use beyond self-acceptance: behaviours are stage-appropriate. Micromanaging ten people made sense; the same behaviour at two hundred bottlenecks the company. "That equation has actually shifted." The cost-benefit makes that visible where *I'm a micromanager* does not.
