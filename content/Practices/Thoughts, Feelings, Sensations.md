# Thoughts, Feelings, Sensations

## What It Is

The simplest practice in Dave's toolkit and the one he ran the longest with Jesse — a three-part check-in that opened every session for roughly two years.

> Right now: thoughts, feelings, sensations.

Three separate registers, reported in order, without interpretation.

## The Model

**Thoughts** — what the mind is saying. *I'm thinking about that meeting we just had about the income statement.*

**Feelings** — the named emotion. *I feel a little anxious, and maybe a little sad.*

**Sensations** — the raw physical event, located. *My eyes feel like they've got some energy behind them.* Andrew's, live in the episode: below the chest, above the stomach, a squeezing.

The separation is the whole point. Most people collapse all three into a single undifferentiated "I'm fine" or "this is bad," and a fused report can't be worked with. Pulling them apart also reveals which layer is generating the trouble — usually the thought. → [[An unresisted emotion moves through the body in 60 to 90 seconds]]

Jesse's account of what it built: he had never noticed his body, never noticed sensations, and consequently "didn't know what emotions were as a result of that."

## How Dave Uses It

As repetition rather than insight — the same three words at the start of every session until the noticing becomes automatic.

Jesse's payoff arrived about six months in, in a meeting where someone presented bad numbers. Instead of the old response, he said out loud: *I feel fear. I notice my chest is getting tight.*

> It was so much more effective than me starting to yell at people for numbers being down.

He is precise about what he'd been doing before, and it wasn't shouting — he'd ask *how come we didn't think to do this? Why aren't we doing it the way they are?* As Andrew notes, you don't have to yell to make someone feel terrible; you just prey on their existing insecurities. → [[Saying I feel fear was more effective than yelling at people]]
