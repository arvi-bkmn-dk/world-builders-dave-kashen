# Emotions and Fear

The claim that founders find hardest to take seriously and that Dave treats as load-bearing: **your capacity to feel is the ceiling on what you can build.** Not a wellbeing argument. A capability one.

- **Emotions and Emotional Fluidity** (364 notes)

## The inherited position

Most founders arrive holding a view they have never examined, because nobody around them examines it either. Business is rational. Emotions are messy, unprofessional, and — as Jesse points out — the charge has long been used to dismiss women. Dave held a softer version of the same thing for most of his life: happiness was the good one, sadness and anger and fear were the bad ones, and anger in particular was what uncivilised people expressed.

The position collapses on contact with how decisions actually get made. People with brain injuries that remove emotional processing cannot choose — not badly, *at all*, taking hours over which pen to use. [[We make decisions emotionally and rationalise them afterwards]] is not a provocation; it is the finding. So emotions are already running every decision in the building. The choice is whether you can read them.

## What an emotion is

Dave's working definition, from Diana Chapman: **energy in motion** → [[Emotion as energy in motion]].

Two things follow. It is **physical** — [[People can access the physical sensation more easily than the feeling]], which is why the practical entry point is the body rather than the label, and why there is a rough map: neck and shoulders for anger, stomach for fear, chest and behind the eyes for sadness, and [[The last line of defence is our jaw|the jaw as the last line of defence]].

And it is **in motion**. [[An unresisted emotion moves through the body in 60 to 90 seconds]]. Jesse's eighteen-month-old cries for twenty-five seconds on being handed over and is then asking for a book. Adults take years, because the movement gets stopped.

Each emotion also arrives carrying a question — [[Each emotion carries its own question, and that is where its wisdom is]]. Anger is boundary energy: *what is not okay here?* Sadness is the energy of letting go: *what is finished?* Fear is the energy of the unknown: *what wants to become known?*

## The single idea the whole topic runs on

**It was never the emotion. It was the resistance to it.**

[[No emotion is painful, the resistance to it is]]. Dave issued this as an on-air correction to his own teaching — having called fear dirty fuel for years, he apologised to his community, because the framing implied fear was the problem. → [[The issue is actually not fear, the issue is resistance to fear]]

The sharpest version comes from Joe Hudson: [[An emotion plus resistance is a different experience from the same emotion alone]]. Not milder — **different in kind**. Which means most people have never met their own sadness or fear, only the resisted composite, and have been drawing conclusions from it. The loop is self-sealing: because we resist them they feel painful, because they feel painful we resist them.

Two things follow that people get wrong. [[The fear that you will drown in a feeling is the lie that keeps it stuck]] — the feeling seems permanent *because* it is being resisted, and the apparent permanence is then read as a reason to keep resisting. And [[You cannot welcome a feeling in order to get rid of it]] — a conditional welcome is resistance wearing the right words, which Dave admits to practising on his own sadness for years.

## What it costs

[[Holding an emotion in costs more energy than feeling it]]. Suppression is not the absence of effort; it is continuous effort, automatic since childhood, and it never appears on the ledger. Dave's comparison is unanswerable: we accept real pain in a gym without argument, then refuse a little constriction in the belly and stay in the marriage.

The costs compound in specific, expensive ways:

- **Decisions stall.** [[More thinking makes clarity worse, like shaking sand in a bottle]]. Clarity is what settling produces; rumination is agitation. Dave's claim is that clarity, not capability, is usually the actual bottleneck.
- **Problems get invented.** [[90% of the time the issues my clients bring are literally not real]] — imagined futures, generated because a present sensation is unwelcome.
- **Boundaries go unset.** [[A leader who cannot feel anger cannot hold a boundary with their team]], and it presents not as anger but as a dull frustration about why people won't do what you want.
- **Nothing ends.** [[Unfelt sadness keeps you in the wrong business, job or relationship]]. Jesse declined a sale because the valuation was below the number in his head, never grieved it, and the enterprise value did not move for five years.
- **Range narrows permanently.** [[All emotions run through the same pipe and joy is the matriarch]]. You cannot block selectively. [[People are more frightened of joy and pleasure than of sadness and anger]].
- **It hardens into character.** [[Personality is a defence structure built from the feelings you would not feel]] — the walls put up to protect us "become our prison when we become adults."

## Shame, which is its own machine

Shame deserves separating out, because it is the mechanism that stops everything else.

[[Shame says I am bad and guilt says I did bad]] — same ingredients, different attachment point. Guilt indicts an action and leaves something to repair; shame indicts the person and leaves nothing to act on.

Joe Hudson supplies the three images that make it usable. [[Shame is the electric fence]] — a training device resting on the premise that we are fundamentally unruly and need correcting. [[Shame puts the brakes on all the other emotions]] — it arrives precisely when something is too intense to feel, which is why welcoming it releases what is underneath. And [[Shame is the lock that binds the chains of bad habits]] — the shame about a habit is its fuel supply, not its consequence.

Which makes the instruction exact: [[Welcome the shame but do not believe the thoughts that come with it]]. Feel the sensation, see through the verdict.

## What to do

- **Drop below the neck.** [[The longest journey is the 18 inches from the head to the heart|Take the elevator from head to heart]]. [[Thoughts, Feelings, Sensations]] is the check-in: thought, feeling, sensation, and most people live in the first third.
- **Assume you are resisting.** Dave's working prior: any feeling you have, you are at least partially resisting.
- **Find what you cannot feel by watching what irritates you.** [[Look at who reacts when babies cry on an airplane|Who reacts when babies cry on an airplane]] → [[What annoys you when others feel it is what you will not feel yourself]].
- **Express it, don't just endure it.** [[Healthy expression is the third option between reacting and holding it in]] — the whiffle ball bat, screaming in the car, shaking like a zebra. Most people have never spent five minutes expressing a feeling.
- **When stuck, ask what feeling the behaviour is avoiding.** [[The behavior is often just a way to avoid feeling some feeling]].
- **If you can't welcome it, welcome the resistance.** [[If you cannot welcome the feeling, welcome the resistance]].

## Why it is a business argument

Your results are limited by which feelings you are unwilling to feel. Emotional capacity problems get misdiagnosed as performance problems. Your personal edges create your company's ceiling.

And the reframe that makes the work attractive rather than remedial: Treat the startup as an emotional intensity gym. The difficulty is the training load. Dave's closing position — *expanding your capacity to be with more and more intense feelings is one of the most important and least valued vectors of growing as a leader.*

On the team side, the payoff is immediate. [[The presenting issue is almost never the real issue]] — 99% of the time, and Jesse's account of replacing the won argument with *you seem angry, what are you mad about* is the most portable move in his work. [[Resolving conflict without allowing the feelings is an insane act]], because the feelings are what generated the conflict.
